\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{The Industrial Paradigm Shift \& Contextualizing OT Constraints}
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Operational Technology (OT) networks form the critical infrastructure backbone of contemporary manufacturing plants, power generation facilities, water treatment utilities, and heavy industrial operations \cite{SunderOT}. Historically, these architectures were physically isolated from external communications through dedicated air-gaps, relying on specialized hardware and vendor-proprietary, closed-loop protocols \cite{StanExtended}. However, the rapid acceleration of Industry 4.0 paradigms, Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) platforms, and centralized manufacturing telemetry has forced a widespread structural convergence between corporate Information Technology (IT) enterprise networks and operational production environments.

While this interconnectivity facilitates real-time supply-chain analytics and automated resource management, directly porting enterprise IT security frameworks into operational technology ecosystems introduces profound systemic friction due to fundamentally opposing operational priorities. In enterprise IT ecosystems, cybersecurity architectures are governed by the classical CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability), wherein data-at-rest encryption, data-in-transit privacy, and access control policies take precedence over operational continuity. 

Within Operational Technology, this hierarchy undergoes a complete structural inversion, transitioning into a paradigm governed by continuous availability, deterministic loop execution, and human/physical safety \cite{SunderOT}. An unauthorized data exposure within an enterprise IT environment incurs regulatory penalties or financial liabilities, but an unplanned shutdown, delayed control loop, or buffer fault in an industrial automation plant can precipitate catastrophic kinetic failures, physical equipment destruction, environmental disasters, and fatal occupational hazards. As a result, standard enterprise strategies, such as routine intrusive vulnerability scanning, automated operating system patching, and reactive fail-closed network terminations, cannot be safely executed on active production lines.

\section{The Evolving Threat Landscape and Operational Urgency in OT}
\label{sec:ot_urgency}
In recent years, the frequency of security advisories and reported vulnerabilities targeting Operational Technology has expanded significantly, reflecting a substantial shift in the adversarial landscape. Industrial cybersecurity monitoring entities, emergency readiness teams such as CISA, and major automation vendors regularly disclose weaknesses across the industrial stack, ranging from supervisory software suites and engineering workstations down to embedded controller firmware and communication stacks \cite{mitre_ics}. While early industrial security incidents were often regarded as rare, highly specialized operations conducted exclusively by advanced nation-state actors, contemporary industrial environments confront a much broader and more commoditized spectrum of risk.

A prominent manifestation of this development is the rise of targeted industrial ransomware, where adversaries no longer confine their campaigns to corporate business tiers but deliberately seek out operational jump hosts, supervisory storage repositories, and central backup systems to halt physical production lines and maximize extortion leverage \cite{SunderOT}. In parallel, common border infrastructure and administrative conduits, such as remote desktop gateways, VPN endpoints, and remote procedure call services operating within the industrial DMZ, are regularly targeted by newly cataloged remote code execution flaws, providing adversaries with straightforward footholds into the automation perimeter \cite{Ou05, TayouriSurvey}. 

Compounding these perimeter risks is the prevalence of native controller and protocol weaknesses that persist within the internal automation architecture \cite{StanExtended}. Beyond traditional memory corruption vulnerabilities in software binaries, security advisories continually document systemic exposures stemming from legacy fieldbus communications, where protocols like Modbus/TCP or standard PROFINET operate without cryptographic session authentication or integrity verification by design \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. Once network reachability is established, an adversary can manipulate operational parameters, forge command frames, or induce denial-of-service states against embedded controllers without needing to exploit complex software bugs \cite{StanExtended}.

The operational urgency to address these exposures stems from the structural dilemma of industrial lifecycles. Heavy manufacturing facilities operate with equipment intended to function reliably for decades, meaning that facilities inherently accumulate technical debt: legacy controllers originally deployed under the assumption of physical isolation are now directly interconnected with supervisory networks and remote maintenance links to satisfy modern data demands. Because these production devices cannot be easily patched, continually upgraded, or actively scanned without introducing severe risks of downtime, plant engineers face an urgent operational requirement for systematic, non-invasive assessment techniques to understand architectural exposure before adversaries can exploit these paths \cite{SunderOT}. Within this context, this thesis does not claim to present an exhaustive solution to industrial cyber risk, but rather proposes an automated, declarative threat modeling pipeline as a conceptual step forward to help practitioners map multi-stage attack paths safely out-of-band \cite{Ou05, alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{The Economic and Physical Risks of Active Vulnerability Testing}
Because industrial automation assets, such as Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs), Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) servers, frequently run legacy embedded software stacks, they exhibit acute structural fragility \cite{SunderOT}. In enterprise IT risk management, practitioners rely on active network scanners (e.g., Nessus, OpenVAS, aggressive \texttt{nmap} sweeps) to probe transport ports, transmit malformed request packets, and extract service banners to discover software Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) \cite{Ou05}.

Within an operational technology environment, however, the high volume of concurrent socket connections, non-standard payload formats, and burst packet rates generated by these active scanners can instantly overwhelm the single-threaded compute engines and finite memory buffers of embedded field devices. This buffer exhaustion can force PLCs into fatal hardware fault modes, disrupt cycle times, or induce control-loop blindness in supervisory SCADA systems. 

Given that unplanned downtime in capital-intensive heavy industries (such as automated steel manufacturing, chemical refining, and automotive production) incurs severe financial penalties and risks severe hardware damage, active penetration testing and intrusive scanning are strictly avoided on operational networks \cite{TayouriSurvey}. Consequently, plant security assessments must be performed entirely out-of-band using non-disruptive, offline modeling techniques prior to physical commissioning or scheduled maintenance windows.

\section{Limitations of Manual Risk Analysis in Large-Scale Industrial Automation}
Compounding the challenge of non-intrusive assessment is the sheer scale and structural complexity of modern industrial facilities. Guided by the ANSI/ISA-95 Purdue Reference Model, these environments are partitioned across hierarchical operational zones \cite{SunderOT}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dozens of virtualized supervisory nodes, engineering workstations (EWS), domain controllers, and process historians operating across Level~3 and the Industrial Demilitarized Zone (IDMZ) \cite{SunderOT}.
    \item Distributed client-server HMIs and manufacturing execution management servers interacting across isolated process cells.
    \item Enterprise-wide Active Directory (AD) forests and identity management services managing plant-wide administrative credentials.
    \item Hundreds of embedded Level~1 PLCs communicating directly with field-level Level~0 actuators, variable frequency drives, and sensors via unauthenticated fieldbus networks \cite{StanExtended}.
\end{itemize}

Evaluating the threat posture of a multi-tiered facility through manual security audits is inefficient, non-exhaustive, and prone to human oversight \cite{KonstaSurvey}. Auditing teams routinely assess assets in isolation, often missing non-obvious, multi-stage propagation paths created by overlapping firewall exceptions, cross-zone routing conduits, and pervasive credential reuse. 

To overcome these manual limitations, the automated synthesis of Logical Attack Graphs (LAGs) is essential \cite{Ou05, TayouriMIRAGE}. By synthesizing network topologies, access control lists (ACLs), host software profiles, and industrial protocol properties into formal declarative logic, automated verification engines can mathematically prove how an adversary can breach the perimeter, pivot through intermediate supervisory layers, and achieve lateral movement to compromise critical physical processes \cite{Ou05, SunderOT}.

\section{Objectives and Scope of the Thesis Research}
\label{sec:objectives_scope}
This research is motivated by two practical realities encountered when attempting security assessments in industrial automation settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Infeasibility of Active Scanning in Operational Environments:} In standard enterprise IT, security assessments rely heavily on automated active scanners such as Nessus, OpenVAS, or broad \texttt{nmap} service sweeps \cite{Ou05}. Applying these active probing tools directly within an Operational Technology facility presents acute operational risks \cite{SunderOT}. Legacy Programmable Logic Controllers, Remote Terminal Units, and embedded fieldbus modules operate with constrained compute resources and single-threaded network stacks. Unsolicited socket connections, burst packet rates, or malformed service probes can exhaust controller buffers, inducing hardware faults, interrupting deterministic control loops, or forcing sudden production downtime. Because live probing cannot be safely executed on active machinery, security assessments must be performed entirely out-of-band by analyzing offline configuration manifests and static asset documentation \cite{TayouriMIRAGE, SunderOT}.

    \item \textbf{The Preponderance of Exploitless Threat Vectors:} Traditional IT threat modeling primarily evaluates software memory corruption vulnerabilities cataloged as Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures \cite{Ou05, TayouriMIRAGE}. In operational environments, however, adversarial propagation rarely relies on complex software exploits once the perimeter is breached \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. Instead, movement across supervisory and control layers is largely enabled by protocol design choices, such as legacy field protocols (Modbus/TCP, PROFINET) that lack cryptographic authentication and integrity verification by design, combined with operational practices like shared administrative credentials across maintenance jump hosts and supervisory nodes \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.
\end{itemize}

Given these constraints, plant engineers often face a dilemma when analyzing risk: manual audits of complex, multi-tiered networks are non-exhaustive and time-consuming, while automated ingestion of semi-structured asset inventories (such as JSON configuration exports) typically forces a difficult choice between brittle regular-expression parsers and unconstrained generative language models prone to ungrounded claims \cite{KonstaSurvey, alviano2025llmasp}.

The objective of this thesis is to explore an automated, out-of-band threat modeling pipeline capable of translating semi-structured network configuration manifests into formal declarative logic to derive multi-stage attack paths without generating live network traffic \cite{Ou05, alviano2025llmasp}. Rather than claiming a universal solution for all industrial architectures, this work focuses specifically on evaluating the feasibility of this pipeline on a realistic plant configuration model provided during an internship at Danieli Automation.

To address this objective, the concrete contributions of this thesis are structured around four focused goals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Neuro-Symbolic Fact Ingestion on an Industrial Case Study:} Investigating a structured division of labor using the LLMASP framework \cite{alviano2025llmasp} applied to a specific industrial schema. Rather than asserting universal plug-and-play cross-vendor interoperability, this work evaluates whether an LLM, constrained strictly to inductive syntactic extraction, guided by plant-specific schema mappings (\texttt{application\_ot.yaml}), and supported by targeted payload slicing, can reliably parse semi-structured configuration manifests into valid relational Datalog atoms without generative hallucinations or syntax collisions. 
    \item \textbf{Declarative Knowledge Completion:} Implementing an Answer Set Programming (ASP) component using Clingo to deduce implicit structural properties, such as pairwise administrative credential sharing, domain controller roles, and unauthenticated protocol bindings, under stable model semantics before submitting facts to the derivation engine \cite{clingo}.
    
    \item \textbf{OT-Specific Logic Formulation:} Extending the MulVAL Datalog reasoning engine with custom interaction rules capturing Purdue Model zone boundaries, credential-based lateral movement, Modbus/TCP command injection, and Layer-2 fieldbus drive manipulation using tabled resolution for predictable execution \cite{Ou05, StanExtended, SunderOT}.
    
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation on the Danieli Plant Model:} Benchmarking the end-to-end pipeline across representative attack goals targeting supervisory services, Level~1 controllers, and physical field drives within the provided Danieli network model. Furthermore, conducting counterfactual experiments to observe whether the logical derivation trees collapse as expected when specific firewall conduits and shared credentials are computationally revoked.
\end{enumerate}

Through this approach, this thesis demonstrates how combining neural semantic extraction with formal declarative solvers can support non-disruptive threat analysis and security planning for a specific, realistic industrial topology without endangering physical plant operations.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}

\section{Security Paradigms: Information Technology vs. Operational Technology}
\label{sec:it_vs_ot}
Understanding the vulnerabilities of Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) requires analyzing the structural divergence between traditional Information Technology (IT) and Operational Technology (OT) ecosystems \cite{SunderOT, StanExtended}. While modern Industry 4.0 initiatives drive interconnectivity between these spaces, their engineering designs, failure modes, and security objectives remain fundamentally distinct.

In enterprise IT systems, cybersecurity architectures are governed by the classical CIA triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability). Data privacy, access authorization, and cryptographic isolation take precedence. IT environments are characterized by relatively homogeneous computing platforms, regular software patching schedules, standardized operating systems, and high tolerance for transient reboots or dynamic connection resets. Security assessments in IT routinely leverage automated, intrusive network scanners, such as Nessus, OpenVAS, or aggressive \texttt{nmap} service sweeps, to transmit thousands of malformed probe packets, identify open transport sockets, and extract software release banners \cite{Ou05}.

Within Operational Technology, this priority hierarchy undergoes an inversion, prioritizing Availability, Deterministic Timing, and Physical Safety over Confidentiality \cite{SunderOT}. OT encompasses supervisory architectures and distributed embedded controllers governing real-world physical dynamics: chemical reactions, high-voltage power routing, and automated steel manufacturing lines \cite{StanExtended}. Unlike enterprise servers, embedded Level~1 Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), and Level~0 variable frequency drives feature single-threaded microcontrollers, fixed cyclic execution loops, and constrained network buffers. 

Subjecting these embedded industrial devices to active IT scanning methodologies precipitates severe physical risks. The high connection volume and packet frequencies generated by standard vulnerability scanners can exhaust controller communication buffers, forcing PLCs into non-recoverable hardware fault states, disrupting millisecond-level scan cycles, or blinding Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs). Because unexpected downtime in capital-intensive manufacturing plants incurs severe financial loss and physical safety hazards, active scanning and intrusive penetration testing are strictly excluded from operational networks \cite{TayouriSurvey}. Security evaluation in OT must therefore be conducted entirely out-of-band through formal, declarative modeling over static configuration manifests and asset inventories \cite{Ou05, TayouriMIRAGE, alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{Attack Trees, Logical Attack Graphs, and Symbolic Representations}
\label{sec:attack_graphs_foundations}

\subsection{The Limitations of Traditional Graph-Theoretic State-Space Models}
The early formalization of automated security analysis in networked environments relied on state-space transition systems \cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}. In these models, security analysis was treated as a verification problem where the entire network infrastructure was modeled as a global finite state machine. Each node in the graph represented an explicit snapshot of the network, capturing the operating system state, patch levels, open ports, and active privilege levels across every host simultaneously. Edges between these state nodes represented discrete atomic state transitions, such as an adversary executing an exploit to transition the network from one global configuration state to another.

While conceptually straightforward, state-space transition systems suffer from a severe computational limitation known as \textit{state-space explosion} \cite{Ou05, WachterSurvey}. Because these frameworks attempt to track all global state permutations, the size of the state graph scales exponentially with the number of networked assets and associated vulnerabilities. For a network containing $H$ distinct hosts, where each host can occupy one of $S$ distinct security configurations, the theoretical state space expands to:
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{S}| = O(S^H)
\end{equation}

When applied to modern industrial automation infrastructures, which routinely comprise dozens of virtualized supervisory nodes, distributed HMIs, engineering workstations, and embedded field controllers, computing the complete state transition graph quickly exceeds memory limits \cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}. This computational bottleneck renders dynamic risk assessment mathematically unfeasible under a classical state-space architecture.

\subsection{Shift to the Monotonic Logic Paradigm and Horn-Clause Datalog}
To bypass the exponential overhead of state-space exploration, modern predictive security modeling transitioned to a formal logic programming paradigm \cite{Ou05, WachterSurvey}. Pioneered by frameworks like MulVAL (Multi-host, Multi-stage Vulnerability Analysis Language), this methodology reformulates threat analysis from state-space searching into declarative theorem proving. Rather than tracking global system states, a logic-based model represents the network through declarative clauses expressed in a subset of first-order logic known as Horn-clause Datalog.

A Horn clause is a disjunction of literals containing at most one positive literal, typically written in rule format as:
\begin{equation}
H \leftarrow B_1 \land B_2 \land \dots \land B_m
\end{equation}
where the head $H$ is derived as a logical consequence if all body subgoals $B_1, \dots, B_m$ evaluate to true \cite{Ou05}.

This paradigm shift relies fundamentally on the principle of \textit{monotonicity} \cite{Ou05}. In a monotonic logic framework, it is assumed that an adversary's acquired privileges and network reachability accumulate over time and are never revoked during the evaluated attack sequence. If an attacker acquires administrative control over a jump server, that privilege remains a true logical fact throughout the duration of the multi-stage evaluation.

By treating attack graph generation as a monotonic derivation problem over Horn clauses, the reasoning engine eliminates the need to maintain a combinatorial graph of explicit system states. Consequently, the computational complexity of attack path synthesis is drastically reduced from exponential time to polynomial time $O(N^2)$, where $N$ represents the total number of independent configuration facts, reachability predicates, and vulnerability assertions in the knowledge base \cite{Ou05}.

\subsection{Taxonomies of Automated Attack Generation Methodologies}
In comprehensive methodological surveys of attack graph formalisms, security researchers categorize automated attack modeling frameworks into three primary generation paradigms \cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pure Graph-Theoretic Architectures:} These systems construct explicit directed graphs to map paths, relying on classical graph traversal algorithms (e.g., Dijkstra, Breadth-First Search) to evaluate reachability. While effective for small networks, they remain constrained by state-space explosion when applied to complex topologies.
    \item \textbf{Model-Checking Frameworks:} Leveraging formal verification engines such as NuSMV or SPIN, these approaches model network components as strict state machines and use temporal logic formulas (e.g., LTL or CTL) to verify safety properties. While mathematically rigorous, they demand substantial memory in large infrastructures.
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Declarative Logic Engines:} Frameworks such as MulVAL leverage Horn-clause logic programming and backward-chaining engines to evaluate security risks \cite{Ou05}. This architecture provides scalability and expressiveness by abstracting network configurations into static relational facts and generic interaction rules, natively maintaining a monotonic view of the environment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Algorithmic Path Discovery \& Scalability Considerations}
As network infrastructures expand to encompass hundreds of interconnected enterprise and operational assets, the total volume of ground configuration facts grows proportionally \cite{AbdellatifAAPDEM}. While monotonic Horn-clause Datalog solvers operate in polynomial time $O(N^2)$, evaluating thousands of rules and predicates across complex corporate-to-OT boundaries can yield extensive derivation trees.

To address computational overhead in large topologies, recent literature has explored specialized path optimization heuristics, including cost-aware $A^*$ search algorithms (such as the AAPDEM model \cite{AbdellatifAAPDEM}) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) pathfinding agents \cite{WachterSurvey}. While these learning-based models provide value for rapid path pruning in hyper-scale cloud environments, they introduce trade-offs in verification explainability and completeness.

In safety-critical industrial automation plants, exhaustive completeness and deterministic formal soundness are mandatory requirements \cite{SunderOT}. Because industrial plant topologies (comprising dozens of servers, engineering workstations, and Level 1 PLCs) remain well within the polynomial scaling envelope of Datalog engines, this research adopts exhaustive, goal-directed backward-chaining powered by XSB Prolog tabling (memoization) \cite{swift2012xsb}. Tabling eliminates redundant re-evaluations and guarantees that all possible adversarial propagation routes are identified deterministically.

\subsection{Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) Context vs. Macroscopic Manifests}
In modern industrial cybersecurity engineering, tracking deployed software components across heterogeneous devices increasingly involves standardized Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) specifications \cite{cyclonedx, spdx}. Frameworks such as CycloneDX (OWASP) and SPDX (ISO/IEC 5962) structure package identifiers, component hierarchies, and cryptographic hashes into machine-readable JSON or XML schemas.

While SBOM standards are essential for tracking third-party component supply chains, operational plant auditing requires macroscopic asset and network manifests that capture holistic site architectures. A component-level SBOM lists firmware packages, but typically omits inter-zone firewall access control lists ($\text{aclNW}$), shared administrative password hashes ($\text{sameCredentials}$), and unauthenticated fieldbus adjacencies ($\text{l2Connection}$). Consequently, this research does not ingest micro-level CycloneDX or SPDX feeds directly; instead, it targets the plant-wide engineering configuration artifact (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation, which functions as an architectural surrogate integrating host parameters, network routing matrices, and vulnerability annotations.

\subsection{Adversary Modeling via the MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS Matrix}
To evaluate industrial cyber-threats beyond abstract reachability graphs, modern security engineering benchmarks attack trajectories against standardized adversary behavioral frameworks \cite{mitre_ics}. Developed specifically to capture operational technology threat vectors, the \textbf{MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS} knowledge base categorizes real-world adversary actions across fourteen distinct tactics, spanning \textit{Initial Access}, \textit{Execution}, \textit{Persistence}, \textit{Privilege Escalation}, \textit{Evasion}, \textit{Lateral Movement}, \textit{Inhibit Response Function}, and \textit{Impair Process Control}.

Unlike enterprise IT attack matrices that emphasize data exfiltration and identity harvesting, ATT\&CK for ICS formalizes domain-specific techniques such as unauthorized command message injection (T0855), controller firmware modification (T0890), and parameter manipulation of physical equipment (T0836) \cite{mitre_ics}. Correlating formal logic interaction rules directly with ATT\&CK for ICS techniques provides a standardized methodology to translate mathematical attack graph proofs into actionable defense-in-depth mitigations.

\section{MulVAL Engine Architecture \& Execution Semantics}
\label{sec:mulval_semantics}
The core execution architecture of MulVAL achieves high scalability by separating the threat model into two distinct, decoupled components: Static Configuration Facts and Generic Interaction Rules \cite{Ou05}.

\subsection{Static Configuration Facts}
These represent the immutable, factual configuration of the target network environment \cite{Ou05}. They are expressed as ground Datalog clauses that register network topology, firewall access control lists (ACLs), software installations, and software vulnerabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{located(Host, Subnet, Type)}: Maps the network topology placement and subnet boundary of an asset.
    \item \texttt{networkService(Host, Program, Protocol, Port, Privilege)}: Defines a listening service binding, port number, and its local execution permissions.
    \item \texttt{vulHost(Host, CVE, Program, ExploitType, Consequence)}: Binds vulnerability intelligence and exploitation consequences to specific hosts.
    \item \texttt{aclNW(Source, Destination, Protocol, Port)}: Represents network filtering, routing reachability, and firewall policies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generic Interaction Rules}
These rules act as the underlying mathematical ``adversarial physics'' that dictate how an attacker can propagate through an environment \cite{Ou05}. These are written as abstracted Horn clauses that define the conditions under which a new logical deduction can be derived from existing facts. For example, a standard remote exploit interaction rule is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{execCode}(\text{Target}, \text{Priv}) \leftarrow \; & \text{networkService}(\text{Target}, \text{Prog}, \text{Prot}, \text{Port}, \text{Priv}), \\
& \text{vulHost}(\text{Target}, \text{CVE}, \text{Prog}, \text{remoteExploit}, \text{privEscalation}), \\
& \text{netAccess}(\text{Principal}, \text{AttackerHost}, \text{Target}, \text{Prot}, \text{Port}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Derivation Engine and Table Optimization}
MulVAL utilizes an underlying XSB Prolog execution engine to perform goal-directed, backward-chaining derivation \cite{swift2012xsb}. Given a user-defined target condition, such as 
\begin{equation}
\texttt{attackGoal(unauthorizedControl(plc\_l1\_1))}
\end{equation}
the engine resolves backward, evaluating which combinations of interaction rules and configuration facts can satisfy the terminal objective.

To optimize this process and prevent non-terminating loops caused by cyclic network dependencies (such as two hosts running identical software and sharing reciprocal network access), the engine leverages XSB Tabling Optimization Directives (e.g., \texttt{:- table execCode/2.}) \cite{swift2012xsb}. Tabling enforces memoization: once a specific security subgoal is evaluated, its truth value is cached in an internal lookup table. If the engine encounters the same subgoal later in another branch of the attack tree, it retrieves the cached result instantly rather than re-evaluating the entire logic sequence. This ensures that the engine dynamically constructs a comprehensive Logical Attack Graph (LAG) while maintaining low polynomial computation times \cite{Ou05}.

\section{Neuro-Symbolic Frontiers: The Emerging Literature}
\label{sec:neuro_symbolic_frontiers}
Bridging the gap between empirical vulnerability discovery and formal reasoning has spurred substantial research combining Large Language Models (LLMs) with symbolic engines \cite{alviano2025llmasp, WachterSurvey}. However, this literature reflects fundamentally different operational goals depending on whether the framework targets active penetration testing or formal, non-intrusive threat modeling.

\subsection{Active LLM-Based Red Teaming vs. Out-of-Band Modeling}
A prominent direction in recent literature focuses on autonomous, active agents designed to conduct live multi-host network penetration testing. The \textbf{Incalmo} framework \cite{SingerIncalmo} addresses long-horizon tactical planning in enterprise networks by employing a hierarchical dual-layer architecture: a high-level LLM planner that tracks compromised subnets, paired with a low-level executor generating live Nmap sweeps, Metasploit payloads, and shell commands. Similarly, \textbf{Aurora} (Wang et al.) \cite{WangSandsToMansions} uses LLMs to extract atomic attack actions from tool documentation and Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) reports, chaining them into causality-preserving multi-step attack scripts using classical Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) solvers. In parallel, frameworks such as \textbf{ATLAS} \cite{ATLASAgent} focus on auditing agent execution traces, applying semantic abstraction and formal automata learning to extract Markov Chains that explain how autonomous agents make decisions.

While active penetration-testing frameworks such as Incalmo and Aurora demonstrate impressive capabilities in enterprise IT environments, their core operational premise, transmitting active exploit payloads, initiating live shell sessions, and brute-forcing authentication, is strictly incompatible with fragile Operational Technology ecosystems \cite{SunderOT, TayouriSurvey}. In an industrial facility, an unguided or semi-autonomous agent executing active scans against an embedded PLC risks triggering buffer overruns and fatal controller halts. Consequently, while active red-teaming literature focuses on automated \textit{execution}, this thesis addresses the complementary requirement of \textbf{out-of-band formal modeling}: generating provably complete attack graphs from static manifests without injecting a single packet into live operational networks.

\subsection{Datalog and Formal Logic Verification in Recent Literature}
Complementing active agents, several frameworks investigate declarative logic to formalize attack paths. \textbf{ATAG} (Gandhi et al.) \cite{GandhiATAG} extends MulVAL's Horn-clause engine to model security risks within Multi-Agent AI Systems (MAAS), formulating custom interaction rules to capture indirect prompt injection and excessive agency cascading across communicating LLM agents. \textbf{EntailLLM} (Mukherji et al.) \cite{MukherjiEntailLLM} addresses the hallucination problem inherent to generative LLMs by evaluating candidate software vulnerability paths through formal Answer Set Programming (ASP) entailment, discarding ungrounded paths that violate language semantics. Furthermore, Hou et al. \cite{HouCTI} investigate translating unstructured CTI reports into Datalog interaction rules, extracting reachable attack chains through monotonic reachability analysis.

\subsection{The RAISE Baseline and Research Gap Closure}
\label{sec:raise_comparison}
A direct baseline for the LLM-to-MulVAL synthesis problem is established by Stevanovic and Wachter in their recent work on automating attack graph construction for agentic pentesting \cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}. Their research investigates the scanner-to-symbolic interoperability problem in web security, presenting a semi-automated two-layer pipeline that parses findings from contemporary scanners (Trivy, Semgrep, Nmap) into MulVAL predicates and employs an LLM-assisted process to construct domain-specific Datalog rules grounded in the MITRE ATT\&CK semantic backbone \cite{mitre_ics}. 

While Stevanovic and Wachter establish the baseline feasibility of extracting MulVAL models via LLM assistance, their empirical evaluation across 54 web Capture-the-Flag tasks exposed significant quality and precision limitations. Specifically, their pipeline achieved a mean vulnerability coverage of only \textbf{53.7\%} (with only \textbf{51.9\%} of tasks achieving full coverage) and produced a substantial mean noise-path rate of \textbf{83.9\%} \cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}. The authors identified that their architecture operated without automated syntactic or semantic validation, requiring manual inspection of generated Datalog rules and human deduplication of predicates, explicitly defining their approach as semi-automated.

This thesis directly closes this identified research gap. Rather than relying on unconstrained LLM rule generation followed by manual post-processing, this research introduces an automated neuro-symbolic pipeline combining the \textbf{LLMASP} methodology \cite{alviano2025llmasp} with \textbf{Clingo (ASP)} and \textbf{MulVAL (Datalog)}. By enforcing strict compile-time syntactic and semantic guardrails, including structured YAML schema slicing, automated lowercasing, arity verification, and stable-model completion, this thesis eliminates generated predicate noise, prevents Prolog variable collisions, and establishes a fully automated, formally sound knowledge extraction pipeline tailored to the constraints of Operational Technology.

\section{The Neuro-Symbolic Ingestion Paradigm (LLMASP Integration)}
\label{sec:llmasp_integration}
While logic-based vulnerability analysis engines provide a scalable framework for evaluating multi-stage attack paths, an automation gap remains in the initial ingestion pipeline \cite{KonstaSurvey, StevanovicWachterRAISE}. MulVAL requires strictly formatted, relational Datalog ground atoms \cite{Ou05}. Real-world industrial environments, however, store asset inventories, firewall access control lists (ACLs), and service mappings in semi-structured JSON profiles, CMDB databases, or engineering spreadsheets \cite{SunderOT}.

To automate this knowledge extraction without relying on brittle, hardcoded regular expression scripts or error-prone unconstrained language models, this research integrates the neuro-symbolic division of labor formalized by Alviano et al. in the LLMASP framework (IJCAI 2025) \cite{alviano2025llmasp}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System-1 (LLM / Syntactic Extraction):} Large Language Models excel at natural language comprehension, pattern recognition, and syntactic entity extraction. In our architecture, the LLM is restricted strictly to syntactic parsing, converting semi-structured JSON objects into explicit Datalog atom strings based on structured schema bounds.
    \item \textbf{System-2 (Answer Set Programming / Formal Reasoning):} ASP solvers (specifically Clingo \cite{clingo}) operate over declarative Horn clauses and disjunctive logic programs under stable model semantics. The ASP engine processes background knowledge rules to infer implicit network relationships (such as credential-sharing links and unauthenticated protocol bindings), verify type constraints, and eliminate logical inconsistencies before facts are submitted to MulVAL.
\end{itemize}

By restricting the LLM to syntactic translation and offloading all logical deduction to an ASP/Datalog engine, the framework mitigates generative hallucinations, prevents predicate arity mismatches, and guarantees compile-time logical soundness \cite{alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{MIRAGE: Micro-Level Firmware Vulnerability Modeling}
\label{sec:mirage_detailed_background}

\subsection{Multi-Binary Firmware Vulnerability Context}
In recent embedded device security research, the MIRAGE (Multi-Binary Image Risk Assessment with Attack Graph Employment) framework introduced by Tayouri et al. represents a foundational milestone in extending Logical Attack Graphs beyond conventional IT networks \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. 

Traditional vulnerability assessment tools analyze IoT and embedded OT edge devices as monolithic entities \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. However, modern embedded devices run multi-binary embedded Linux distributions (e.g., OpenWrt, BusyBox environments) where operational tasks, such as HTTP web servers, UPnP daemons, DNS forwarders, and proprietary industrial protocol parsers, are partitioned across disparate executable binaries. These distinct binaries execute concurrently under varying privilege boundaries and communicate internally via Inter-Process Communication (IPC) channels, including UNIX domain sockets, FIFO named pipes, shared memory segments, and environment variables.

Tayouri et al. demonstrated that evaluating vulnerabilities within isolated binaries produces severe blind spots \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. An adversary who successfully exploits an initial memory corruption bug in an exposed, network-facing binary (e.g., a lightweight web server) rarely achieves direct root execution immediately. Instead, the attacker initiates an \textit{intranode lateral pivot}: by manipulating local IPC data streams or exploiting shared dynamic libraries (e.g., vulnerable \texttt{libuClibc} routines), the adversary propagates execution across internal binary boundaries, progressively escalating privileges until full device takeover is achieved.

\subsection{The Four-Stage MIRAGE Architecture}
To model intra-device vulnerability chaining, MIRAGE structures risk assessment into a sequential four-stage static analysis pipeline \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Modeler (Firmware Decomposition):} Unpacks compressed firmware filesystem images (SquashFS, CramFS, JFFS2) using Binwalk. It performs static analysis to chart border binaries (daemons bound to external TCP/UDP sockets) and maps internal IPC data dependencies across local binaries.
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability Mapper (NVD Heuristics \& QEMU Emulation):} Identifies software Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) affecting individual binaries. It queries the NIST National Vulnerability Database (NVD) using static indicator heuristics, version string scraping, and QEMU-emulated command-line execution to emit \texttt{vulExists/5} predicates.
    \item \textbf{Attack Graph Generator (Intranode Logic Engine):} Ingests extracted IPC dependencies and \texttt{vulExists} assertions into an extended MulVAL engine. MIRAGE defines specialized interaction rules that formalize how memory corruption in one binary transfers tainted control flow to adjacent binaries over local IPC channels.
    \item \textbf{Attack Graph Analyzer (EPSS Risk Scoring):} Computes risk scores across derived attack paths by combining binary interaction frequency with Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) probability metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Theoretical and Methodological Disconnect: Realigning from Firmware to Plant Networks}
\label{sec:mirage_disconnect}
While MIRAGE successfully applied MulVAL attack graphs to embedded firmware, its architecture cannot be ported directly to solve the threat modeling challenge targeted in this thesis. A fundamental methodological, structural, and semantic gap exists between MIRAGE's micro-level firmware objectives and our macroscopic industrial plant requirements. 

In terms of spatial abstraction, MIRAGE operates strictly at the \textbf{micro-level within the single-device execution space} of a system-on-chip, analyzing Linux kernel IPC tables, UNIX domain sockets, and shared memory segments \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In contrast, our industrial framework operates across a \textbf{macro-level plant footprint}, evaluating multi-tier Purdue Model architectures spanning 21+ virtualized servers, domain controllers, PLCs, and physical drives segmented by inter-zone firewall ACLs (\texttt{aclNW/4}) and Layer-2 fieldbuses (\texttt{l2Connection/5}) \cite{SunderOT}. 

From an ingestion standpoint, MIRAGE relies on \textbf{binary firmware decompilation via Binwalk} and static string scraping \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In production industrial manufacturing facilities, security teams do not possess the source firmware binaries for proprietary, vendor-locked controllers; instead, they manage high-level configuration management databases and semi-structured JSON asset sheets, requiring our pipeline to introduce \textbf{neuro-symbolic semantic extraction (LLMASP)} \cite{alviano2025llmasp}. 

Furthermore, a significant divergence exists across the vulnerability spectrum. MIRAGE is built upon a \textbf{pure software CVE model}, querying the NVD to evaluate memory corruption bugs \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In operational technology networks, this assumption is fundamentally incomplete, as controllers and drives are routinely compromised without software vulnerabilities. Our framework establishes a \textbf{hybrid dual-class vulnerability engine}, modeling software CVEs alongside exploitless protocol flaws (unauthenticated Modbus/TCP, PROFINET) and systemic credential reuse \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. 

This distinction naturally alters the underlying deductive logic: while MIRAGE traces \textbf{intranode binary taint propagation} over local shared libraries, our pipeline deduces \textbf{multi-hop Purdue zone traversals}, Active Directory domain takeovers, and fieldbus master-slave hijacking. 

Finally, the analytical metrics and operational objectives diverge completely. For MIRAGE, the terminal evaluation calculates probabilistic vulnerability prioritization via Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) metrics and local root privilege escalation \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. 

In our operational plant setting, numerical EPSS scoring is discarded for two reasons. First, EPSS models rely on empirical exploit observation in the wild, which does not exist for Class~II exploitless protocol flaws (e.g., standard Modbus function codes or Layer-2 PROFINET commands). Second, safety-critical industrial operations demand deterministic guarantees rather than probabilistic likelihoods. Consequently, our framework replaces numerical EPSS scoring with formal causal trace extraction and binary counterfactual verification (evaluating whether target goals transition deterministically between $\mathbf{DERIVABLE}$ and $\mathbf{BLOCKED}$ under proposed mitigations).

\section{OT Networks and the Purdue Reference Model}
\label{sec:purdue_model}
To isolate fragile automation equipment from volatile business enterprise networks, industrial architectures are structured according to the Purdue Reference Model for ICS Security (ANSI/ISA-95) \cite{SunderOT}. This hierarchical architecture organizes industrial plant networks into distinct operational zones:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 4 / Enterprise Network:} Hosts enterprise resource planning (ERP), corporate database systems, and business management services.
    \item \textbf{Level 3.5 / Industrial DMZ (IDMZ):} Provides a security barrier containing jump servers, reverse proxies, and patch management servers to terminate direct network sessions between enterprise and automation environments.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 / Operations Management:} Houses plant-wide supervisory control servers, data historians, engineering workstations (EWS), and domain controllers managing operational authorization.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 / Control Systems:} Encompasses supervisory control networks, local Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs), and localized SCADA operator terminals.
    \item \textbf{Level 1 / Basic Control:} Contains Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), and safety instrumented systems executing real-time control algorithms.
    \item \textbf{Level 0 / Physical Process:} Comprises physical actuators, sensors, motors, variable frequency drives, and physical field instrumentation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Domain-Specific Logic Extensions for Cyber-Physical Systems}
\label{sec:cps_logic_extensions}
While foundational logic engines like MulVAL revolutionized automated threat modeling, their standard rule sets are inherently constrained to traditional IT abstractions \cite{Ou05, StanExtended}. In a standard IT security model, system interactions are generalized around standard operating system permissions (e.g., \texttt{user}, \texttt{admin}, \texttt{root}), network connections established over standard TCP/IP sockets, and software vulnerabilities cataloged exclusively via the CVE index.

However, this abstraction fails when applied to the operational realities of Cyber-Physical Systems \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. Industrial environments incorporate thousands of embedded field devices that lack standard operating systems or user account management. In these networks, compromise does not merely manifest as user-to-root privilege escalation; instead, it frequently involves the unauthorized injection of unauthenticated control commands directly into a physical process loop. To capture realistic multi-stage adversarial pathways within industrial infrastructures, formal logic threat models must be extended with domain-specific interaction rules that natively capture network hierarchies, industrial protocol behaviors, and cyber-physical dependencies \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.

\section{MulVAL's Limitations in Industrial Environments}
\label{sec:mulval_limitations}
Standard vulnerability scanners and IT-centric logic engines exhibit blind spots when analyzing industrial network configurations \cite{SunderOT}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Individual Asset Assessment:} They evaluate assets in isolation, assuming that the absence of a known software CVE equates to a secure operational state \cite{Ou05}.
    \item \textbf{Flat Topology Assumptions:} Traditional logic engines treat network filtering policies as simple end-to-end reachability checks, failing to capture the cascading consequences that zone-to-zone firewall policies impose across multi-tier Purdue layers \cite{SunderOT}.
    \item \textbf{Protocol Blindness:} Traditional IT logic structures lack predicates to express the unauthenticated and unencrypted landscape of legacy industrial communications pervasive across lower architectural layers \cite{StanExtended}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Modeling Legacy Industrial Protocols and Exploitless Vulnerabilities}
\label{sec:legacy_protocols}
A critical security risk in OT environments stems from the pervasive reliance on legacy automation protocols, such as Modbus/TCP, PROFINET, and EtherNet/IP, which were designed without cryptographic authentication, integrity checks, or encryption \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. Consequently, any network node that achieves logical or physical proximity to an industrial bus can execute entirely exploitless compromises, such as spoofing control messages, rewriting PLC register parameters, or issuing force-shutdown commands to field devices.

To capture these protocol characteristics within a formal logic framework, the threat model introduces custom interaction rules representing vulnerabilities in the communication protocol itself, rather than software implementation errors (CVEs) \cite{StanExtended}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
vulLinkProtocol(sub_proc_area_1, vul_modbus_no_auth, modbus, adjacent, impersonateDst).
\end{lstlisting}

When an adversary achieves network reachability to an unauthenticated controller port, the logic engine evaluates this as an immediate command injection pathway:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
interaction_rule(
  (unauthorizedControl(TargetPLC) :-
    execCode(SupervisorHost, admin),
    aclNW(SupervisorHost, TargetPLC, tcp, 502),
    networkService(TargetPLC, _Service, tcp, 502, _Perm)),
  rule_desc('Unauthorized command injection into PLC over unauthenticated Modbus/TCP', 0.9)
).
\end{lstlisting}

This extended rule allows the engine to mathematically prove how a compromised engineering workstation or administrative jump server can bypass traditional security barriers and manipulate physical field equipment directly over unauthenticated operational buses \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.

\section{Access Control Matrices and Zone-to-Zone Cross-Talk Logic}
\label{sec:access_control_matrices}
Because network segmentation via industrial firewalls represents the primary line of defense in an OT infrastructure, the logic model enforces a strict network filtering matrix \cite{SunderOT}. In an industrial plant, network access is constrained: assets in a Level 2 supervisory network are restricted from interacting directly with Level 0/1 field equipment, except through explicitly authorized, tightly restricted firewall conduits (e.g., an HMI client polling a specific PLC over a dedicated industrial port).

Extended Datalog models represent this structural access control matrix using explicit network access tokens (\texttt{aclNW/4}), which define the exact permissible paths through firewalls \cite{Ou05, SunderOT}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
aclNW(jump_server, vdc01, tcp, 445).
aclNW(jump_server, plc_l1_1, tcp, 502).
\end{lstlisting}

The reasoning engine processes these firewall permissions alongside custom crosstalk and credential reuse rules to evaluate lateral movement vectors:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
interaction_rule(
  (execCode(TargetHost, admin) :- 
    execCode(CompromisedHost, admin),
    sameCredentials(CompromisedHost, TargetHost)),
  rule_desc('Lateral movement across zones via shared administrative credentials', 1.0)
).
\end{lstlisting}

Through this rule-based approach, the extended logic engine maps how configuration oversights, shared administrative credentials, and unauthorized crosstalk conduits combine to create critical threat vectors, providing a formal framework to assess and harden industrial networks against multi-stage cyberattacks \cite{Ou05, StanExtended, SunderOT}.

\section{Identified Engineering \& Methodological Problems}
\label{sec:identified_problems}
Building an automated bridge between the plant configuration manifests from Danieli Automation and a formal logic engine reveals five practical challenges. These problems stem from the nature of industrial data storage, token and memory constraints, relational complexity, domain differences in communication protocols, and execution loops in formal solvers.

\subsection{Problem 1: Structural and Syntactic Mismatch in Data Representations}
The first obstacle is the representational gap between how industrial networks are documented and what formal reasoning engines require. 

In modern industrial facilities, asset inventories and network policies are stored in semi-structured, nested formats, such as JSON, CSV tables, or database dumps, to make them easy for human engineers to read and maintain. In the Danieli plant manifest, each machine is an object containing nested arrays for installed software, credential mappings, and firewall conduits, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:json_sample}. 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=json, caption={Sample Raw Industrial Asset Profile from \texttt{configuration.json}}, label={lst:json_sample}]
{
  "id": "jump_server",
  "zone": "ot_dmz",
  "credentials": {
    "admin": "admin_hash_1969"
  },
  "software_services": [
    {
      "name": "sshd",
      "port": 22,
      "protocol": "tcp",
      "privilege": "admin",
      "cve": "cve_2023_38408"
    }
  ],
  "firewall_rules": [
    { "destination": "vdc01", "port": 445, "protocol": "tcp" },
    { "destination": "plc_l1_1", "port": 502, "protocol": "tcp" }
  ]
}
\end{lstlisting}

In contrast, declarative logic tools like MulVAL require a flat relational model composed strictly of typed Datalog ground atoms:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
located(jump_server, ot_dmz, ipSubnet).
hasAccount(jump_server, admin, admin_hash_1969).
networkService(jump_server, sshd, tcp, 22, admin).
vulHost(jump_server, cve_2023_38408, sshd, remoteExploit, privEscalation).
aclNW(jump_server, vdc01, tcp, 445).
aclNW(jump_server, plc_l1_1, tcp, 502).
\end{lstlisting}

Converting one format into the other is difficult to automate reliably:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brittleness of Deterministic Scripts:} Standard procedural scripts relying on hardcoded keys and regular expressions fail whenever documentation formats shift. If an engineer renames a field from \texttt{software\_services} to \texttt{installed\_apps}, changes port values from numbers to strings, or alters the nesting depth of firewall rules, deterministic parsers crash or drop records silently.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic Errors in Generative Models:} Using an unconstrained Large Language Model to write Datalog directly introduces subtle syntax errors. In Prolog and Datalog syntax, identifiers beginning with an uppercase letter are automatically treated as unbound variables rather than constant entities. If a model outputs \texttt{located(Jump\_Server, Ot\_dmz, Subnet)}, the engine interprets the entire statement as an ungrounded variable pattern rather than a concrete host on a subnet, causing logical failures during path derivation. Furthermore, generative models frequently misalign the number of arguments (arity) in standard predicates or hallucinate connections that do not exist in the source file.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problem 2: Context Window Saturation and API Extraction Limits}
The second problem involves the volume of data that must be processed when analyzing a realistic facility. A plant like the Danieli testbed comprises 21 interconnected assets, dozens of services, and a comprehensive cross-zone firewall matrix.

When a complete configuration file is sent to a language model in a single prompt, two issues arise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attentional Degradation across Long Contexts:} While large language models support wide input windows, their accuracy in extracting dense, structured data decreases over long contexts. When presented with a single monolithic JSON document containing hundreds of lines of firewall rules and host objects, models tend to skip isolated attributes, such as a single credential entry, an internal port exception, or a fieldbus identifier.
    \item \textbf{Token Consumption and Rate Limiting:} Repeatedly querying an API with an entire monolithic document to extract different aspects of the plant quickly exhausts token quotas and triggers API rate limits, adding unnecessary latency to the pipeline.
\end{itemize}
The pipeline therefore requires a method to break the input data down into smaller, targeted slices without losing the contextual connections between hosts and their network rules.

\subsection{Problem 3: Combinatorial Overhead in Cross-Host Account Matching}
A frequent lateral movement vector in industrial networks is administrative credential reuse. Plant engineers often use the same local administrative password across jump boxes, domain controllers, and auxiliary workstations to simplify maintenance.

To represent this attack surface formally, the system must declare which hosts share identical credentials using a relation such as \texttt{sameCredentials(HostA, HostB)}. However, determining every instance of credential reuse requires evaluating all pairs of hosts across the network. For an inventory of size $|\mathcal{H}|$, the number of pairwise comparisons grows quadratically:
\begin{equation}
\frac{|\mathcal{H}| \times (|\mathcal{H}| - 1)}{2} = O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)
\end{equation}

Relying on a generative language model to inspect an entire inventory and manually enumerate every matching pair is error-prone. Generative models struggle with exhaustive combinatorial comparisons across large sets, frequently omitting valid matches or inventing pairings that do not share hashes. On the other hand, requiring human plant operators to manually write down every pair in advance defeats the purpose of an automated pipeline. The extraction of basic facts must therefore be decoupled from the combinatorial logic needed to discover shared credentials.

\subsection{Problem 4: Modeling Exploitless Protocols and Industrial Device Realities}
Traditional vulnerability analysis tools were designed around enterprise IT environments. In an IT network, an attacker typically moves from one system to another by exploiting memory corruption bugs (such as buffer overflows in software binaries cataloged as CVEs) to escalate privileges from a basic user to an administrator or root.

In an industrial plant, this abstraction fails to capture how control systems operate and how physical processes are manipulated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Operating System Privileges:} Many lower-tier devices, such as Level~1 Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) and Level~0 variable frequency drives, run dedicated, embedded firmware without standard multi-user operating systems or login accounts. An adversary does not need to escalate privileges from ``user'' to ``root'' on these controllers.
    \item \textbf{Exploitless Communication Protocols:} Core operational protocols like Modbus/TCP (port 502) and standard PROFINET operate without cryptographic session authentication, packet integrity checks, or encryption by design. Once an adversary obtains network access to the controller's listening port through a firewall exception, they can send valid supervisory commands directly to the hardware. They can alter process setpoints, force emergency stops, or rewrite register values without exploiting any software flaw.
    \item \textbf{Layer-2 Fieldbus Hierarchies:} Actuators and drives on Level~0 are managed directly by PLCs acting as bus masters over Layer-2 industrial networks. Standard IT attack rules do not account for master-slave bus relationships where compromising the controller automatically grants control over downstream physical equipment.
\end{itemize}
Traditional Datalog interaction rules must therefore be extended with domain-specific logic to represent these exploitless protocol realities alongside classical software CVEs.

\subsection{Problem 5: Termination Guarantees and Cyclic Paths in Network Graphs}
The final challenge lies in the execution of the derivation engine itself. Industrial plants frequently include symmetric network access policies, reciprocal connections between supervisory nodes, and mirrored jump hosts for redundancy.

When these relationships are translated into logical rules, such as bidirectional reachability or mutual credential sharing, standard backward-chaining evaluation (like SLD resolution in basic Prolog) can enter infinite loops. If Host $A$ shares credentials with Host $B$, the engine may attempt to prove that Host $A$ can compromise Host $B$, which can compromise Host $A$, leading to non-terminating recursion. 

Without explicit memoization and loop control mechanisms, the derivation engine cannot guarantee termination, preventing the complete synthesis of an attack graph. The reasoning framework must therefore enforce structural tabling to ensure that recursive path exploration terminates reliably in polynomial time.

\chapter{System Architecture \& Methodology}
\label{chap:architecture}

To address the representational and operational challenges outlined in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, this thesis explores an automated, out-of-band threat modeling pipeline. Rather than relying on live network probing or unconstrained language models, the approach combines prompt-constrained syntactic extraction, declarative Answer Set Programming, and tabled Horn-clause logic derivation. This chapter defines the underlying threat model, establishes the mathematical system formalisms, and walks through each phase of the methodology, discussing the practical rationales behind each design decision.

\section{Formal Attacker and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat_model}
To establish mathematically verifiable security bounds, we define an explicit threat model governing the adversary's capabilities, initial access, and lateral progression boundaries across the industrial plant topology.

\subsection{Adversary Profile and Capabilities}
We model an external, computationally bounded adversary originating outside the plant's operational perimeter. The adversary's initial execution capabilities are restricted strictly to the untrusted network perimeter, formally asserted as $\text{attackerLocated}(\text{internet}) \land \text{malicious}(\text{internet})$. 

In terms of initial intelligence, the adversary possesses no prior cryptographic credentials, authentication tokens, or internal topological maps, but is assumed capable of conducting reconnaissance against externally reachable endpoints, such as administrative ports exposed through the Industrial Demilitarized Zone (IDMZ). The adversary is equipped with functional exploits for publicly cataloged software vulnerabilities (Class~I CVEs) affecting these exposed services. 

Once an intermediate host $H \in \mathcal{H}$ is compromised, achieving administrative execution denoted as $\text{execCode}(H, \text{admin})$, the adversary inherits the reachability permissions granted to that host by the plant firewall access control matrix. Furthermore, gaining control of a host provides access to its local credential storage, enabling lateral credential reuse across any systems that share identical password hashes, represented by $\text{sameCredentials}(H, H')$ relations. 

Finally, upon acquiring either administrative execution or unauthenticated network reachability to an industrial controller, the adversary can leverage native field protocol characteristics (Class~II flaws). This allows the adversary to transmit unauthenticated command frames across Modbus/TCP or Layer-2 PROFINET links to alter setpoints or manipulate physical field equipment without needing software memory exploits.

\subsection{Adversary Objective Function}
The objective of the adversary is to transition the industrial plant from a nominal operating state into one or more unauthorized operational states within a target set $\mathcal{G}_{target} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_D$. Formally, the threat model evaluates whether the logical derivation engine can satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    \exists \tau = \langle r_1, r_2, \dots, r_k \rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{F}_{complete} \vdash_{\tau} g, \quad g \in \mathcal{G}_{target}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ represents a finite sequence of interaction rule applications from the domain ruleset $\mathcal{R}_{OT}$. These derivations evaluate whether the adversary can attain terminal goals such as Active Directory compromise ($\text{domainCompromise}(\text{danieli\_domain})$), unauthorized command injection into plant controllers ($\text{unauthorizedControl}(\text{plc\_l1\_1})$), or direct manipulation of variable frequency drives ($\text{physicalDriveControl}(\text{jump\_server}, \text{drive\_area\_1\_1})$).

\subsection{Trust Assumptions and Security Invariants}
The formal verification model relies on three structural invariants:
First, we enforce a \textit{Firewall Enforcement Invariant}, assuming that network filtering hardware deterministically enforces the access control matrix $\mathcal{A}_{CL}$ declared in configuration manifests, such that network traffic cannot cross zone boundaries unless explicitly permitted by an $\text{aclNW}(Src, Dst, Prot, Port)$ clause. 
Second, we reflect \textit{Embedded Device Fragility} by recognizing that Level~1 controllers and Level~0 fieldbus drives operate without protocol-layer cryptographic authentication; thus, any host capable of routing packets to an industrial port is trusted by the controller to issue operational commands. 
Third, following standard logic programming conventions, the framework assumes \textit{Monotonic Privilege Accumulation}, meaning that privileges, access tokens, and reachability acquired during an intrusion sequence remain valid throughout the multi-stage evaluation.

\section{Mathematical System Model \& Multi-Layer Graph Formalism}
\label{sec:system_model}
We represent the industrial plant infrastructure as a multi-tier cyber-physical system model, structured around formal relational sets.

\subsection{Industrial Plant Model Formalization}
The industrial automation plant is formalized as a 5-tuple:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S} = (\mathcal{H}, \mathcal{Z}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{F}_{bus}, \mathcal{A}_{CL})
\end{equation}
The set $\mathcal{H} = \{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_n\}$ represents the finite collection of physical and virtual host entities, including supervisory servers, domain controllers, operator workstations, industrial controllers, and field drives. 

The spatial partitioning of the plant is defined by $\mathcal{Z} = \{\text{internet}, \text{ot\_dmz}, \text{danieli\_domain}, \text{level\_2\_supervisory}, \text{level\_1\_network}, \text{fieldbus\_l0}\}$, which maps directly to the functional security zones of the Purdue Reference Model via a surjective mapping function $\zeta: \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{Z}$. 

Active transport services are represented by the relation $\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{S}_{prog} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot} \times \mathbb{N} \times \mathcal{P}_{riv}$, where each element $(h, s, p, port, priv)$ specifies an active listening daemon $s$ bound to protocol $p \in \{\text{tcp}, \text{udp}\}$ on transport port $port$, executing under privilege level $priv \in \{\text{user}, \text{admin}, \text{root}\}$. 

Physical industrial communications at lower layers are captured by $\mathcal{F}_{bus} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{B}_{id} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot}$, defining Layer-2 fieldbus connections between master controllers and slave actuators. 

Finally, the network access control policy is defined by the matrix $\mathcal{A}_{CL} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot} \times \mathbb{N}$, which formalizes the permissible communication channels established across inter-zone firewalls.

\subsection{Bipartite Logical Attack Graph Semantics}
The threat modeling pipeline compiles these environmental relations into a directed bipartite Logical Attack Graph (LAG):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}_{AND} \cup \mathcal{V}_{OR}, \mathcal{E})
\end{equation}
The vertices of the graph are partitioned into two distinct semantic classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Derivation Nodes ($\mathcal{V}_{AND}$):} These represent instantiated interaction rules from the extended domain logic $\mathcal{R}_{OT}$. An AND-node is logically satisfied if and only if all of its incoming precondition child nodes evaluate to true:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{val}(v_{AND}) = \bigwedge_{(u, v_{AND}) \in \mathcal{E}} \text{val}(u)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{State Nodes ($\mathcal{V}_{OR}$):} These denote either primitive configuration facts ($v \in \mathcal{V}_P$) or derived privilege states ($v \in \mathcal{V}_D$). An OR-node is satisfied if at least one incoming derivation rule is satisfied:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{val}(v_{OR}) = \bigvee_{(v_{AND}, v_{OR}) \in \mathcal{E}} \text{val}(v_{AND})
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 1: Targeted Payload Slicing and Syntactic Ingestion}
The first phase of the pipeline ingests the semi-structured industrial configuration manifest (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation. Submitting an entire industrial manifest into a single large language model prompt often leads to token limits and attentional degradation across long contexts. Conversely, deterministic scripts break when field names or object hierarchies change across plant management tools.

To address this challenge, our architecture adapts the neuro-symbolic division of labor from the LLMASP framework. Here, the language model is restricted strictly to an inductive syntactic parser (System-1). Rather than passing the full JSON file on every query, the pipeline controller (\texttt{run\_llmasp.py}) applies \textit{targeted payload slicing}. 

The controller reads the extraction targets declared in \texttt{application\_ot.yaml} and extracts only the specific subtrees required for each target relation. For instance, when parsing network reachability rules (\texttt{aclNW/4}), the prompt receives only the \texttt{firewall\_rules} array. When extracting physical Layer-2 fieldbuses (\texttt{l2Connection/5}), only the \texttt{fieldbus\_connections} block is submitted. Similarly, when querying host-level attributes, such as zone placement, listening services, and local credential hashes, the model receives only the pruned \texttt{hosts} array. This slicing strategy reduces per-request prompt volume by up to 65\%, keeping token counts well within API limits and preventing the model from missing isolated configuration attributes.

\subsection{Step 2: Semantic Normalization and Syntactic Guardrails}
Because generative models can introduce subtle formatting inconsistencies, the pipeline intercepts all output emitted by System-1 prior to any logical reasoning. In Datalog and Prolog semantics, any identifier starting with a capital letter is treated as an unbound variable rather than an atomic constant. If an extraction query produces an atom like \texttt{located(Jump\_Server, Ot\_dmz, IpSubnet)}, the reasoning engine fails to ground the host to a specific zone, invalidating subsequent derivations.

The pipeline controller enforces automated \textit{compile-time guardrails} to prevent these syntax issues. All extracted strings, host names, and protocol identifiers are automatically converted into lowercase alphanumeric characters separated by underscores. 

In addition, the controller validates the arity and type signatures of each extracted atom against expected schemas, verifying that predicates like \texttt{networkService} contain exactly five arguments and that transport ports are valid numerical values. Any malformed, partial, or out-of-schema outputs are discarded immediately, producing a clean, verified base of explicit facts ($\mathcal{F}_{explicit}$).

\subsection{Step 3: Declarative Knowledge Completion via Clingo}
After explicit facts are parsed, the system must derive the implicit architectural relationships across the plant. As established in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, calculating administrative password sharing across an entire network scales quadratically ($O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)$) with the number of hosts. Prompting a generative language model to enumerate every valid pair across 21 interconnected machines introduces severe hallucination risks and omitted links.

Rather than relying on neural inference for relational completion, this step is offloaded to an Answer Set Programming solver, specifically Clingo~5.x (System-2A). Operating under the Closed World Assumption with stable model semantics, Clingo processes declarative background knowledge rules ($\mathcal{K}_{KB}$):
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
sameCredentials(H1, H2) :- 
    hasAccount(H1, U, P), 
    hasAccount(H2, U, P), 
    H1 != H2.

isDomainController(H, danieli_domain) :- 
    networkService(H, active_directory, _, _, _).

vulLinkProtocol(H, vul_modbus_no_auth, modbus, adjacent, impersonateDst) :- 
    networkService(H, modbus_service, _, _, _).

isMaster(H, bus_area_1) :- 
    networkService(H, modbus_service, tcp, 502, _).

isSlave(D, bus_area_1) :- 
    located(D, fieldbus_l0, _).
\end{lstlisting}

By executing this logic declaratively, Clingo evaluates all pairwise credential matches, classifies Domain Controllers, and formalizes unauthenticated protocol bindings in approximately 0.0060 seconds with formal soundness. This step eliminates manual pairing overhead while preventing generative hallucinations. The resulting stable model is then combined with static axioms to materialize the closed Datalog knowledge base, saved as \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}.

\subsection{Step 4: Monotonic Derivation with MulVAL and Tabled XSB}
In the third phase, the materialized fact base is ingested by the MulVAL reasoning engine (System-2B), executed on an underlying XSB Prolog environment. 

MulVAL evaluates the completed environment against our extended domain ruleset, \texttt{attack1.P}. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, real-world industrial configurations often feature reciprocal firewall access, bidirectional communication conduits, or mutual credential sharing. Under standard Prolog SLD resolution, these symmetric relationships cause infinite backward-chaining loops. 

To guarantee termination, the execution environment enforces \textit{XSB Tabling Directives} on intermediate derived predicates, such as \texttt{:- table execCode/2.} and \texttt{:- table netAccess/5.}. Tabling provides memoization: once a specific subgoal is derived for a host or zone, its truth value is cached internally. When the backward-chaining engine encounters that subgoal again through an alternate or cyclical path, it retrieves the cached result directly rather than re-evaluating the rule tree. This ensures that cyclic reachability paths terminate reliably and that attack graphs compile in low polynomial time ($O(N^2)$).

\subsection{Step 5: Graph Materialization and Counterfactual Hardening}
The final phase of the pipeline formats the logical derivation trees into practical security artifacts. The engine outputs graphical attack trees in PDF and Graphviz DOT formats for visual inspection, generates detailed textual execution traces mapping the step-by-step path from perimeter to target, and logs execution timing benchmarks.

Beyond diagnosing vulnerabilities, the pipeline functions as a tool for \textit{counterfactual verification}. By adjusting specific properties in the initial JSON configuration, such as revoking a direct maintenance firewall conduit or replacing shared administrative passwords with unique credentials, plant engineers can re-run the pipeline to observe whether the corresponding attack paths collapse. This allows security teams to verify the effectiveness of proposed mitigations mathematically before executing any configuration changes on live machinery.

\section{Architectural Lineage and Design Rationales}
\label{sec:design_rationales}

\subsection{Methodological Lineage: Adapting the Four-Stage Shape}
The organizational workflow of our pipeline follows the four-stage progression established in recent attack graph literature, particularly the MIRAGE framework: asset modeling, vulnerability mapping, interaction derivation, and path analysis \cite{TayouriMIRAGE}.

However, as analyzed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, MIRAGE was engineered for micro-level firmware auditing, decomposing individual device binary images using Binwalk and QEMU-emulated CVE version lookups. In contrast, our pipeline adapts this four-stage structure to macroscopic industrial plant topologies with three deliberate architectural departures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We replace binary firmware decompilation with prompt-constrained semantic extraction over semi-structured configuration manifests (LLMASP).
    \item We replace pure software CVE databases with a dual-class vulnerability model that incorporates unauthenticated field protocols and systemic credential reuse.
    \item We replace probabilistic EPSS risk scoring with deterministic counterfactual satisfiability. Because statistical exploit prediction scores do not exist for unauthenticated protocol interactions (such as native Modbus/TCP or Layer-2 PROFINET commands), our fourth stage focuses on formal logical verification, proving mathematically whether designated mitigations break the derivation trees across the Purdue hierarchy.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Why LLMASP over End-to-End Fine-Tuned LLMs?}
A possible alternative architecture would involve fine-tuning an end-to-end language model to ingest the configuration file and directly output attack graphs in Graphviz DOT format. We explicitly chose not to use an end-to-end neural approach based on three practical considerations:

First, generative language models operate probabilistically, predicting output tokens based on statistical likelihood rather than formal mathematical deduction. When tasked with multi-hop reachability reasoning across complex networks, they frequently hallucinate plausible-looking attack paths that violate underlying firewall rules. In industrial automation, where assessments inform operational safety, threat models must be formally sound and verifiable.

Second, configuration schemas vary across automation vendors, such as differences between Siemens TIA Portal outputs, Rockwell manifests, and Danieli Automation inventories. An end-to-end fine-tuned model overfits to specific naming conventions and requires retraining when fields change. In contrast, prompt-guided semantic extraction (System-1) adapts dynamically to different schema layouts through lightweight prompt adjustments without model retraining.

Third, safety-critical industrial operations require explainability. End-to-end neural generation functions as a black box, making it difficult to audit why a particular path was generated. In our neuro-symbolic framework, all causal reasoning is delegated to a deterministic logic engine, ensuring that every transition in the derived attack graph is supported by a formal Horn-clause derivation trace.

\subsection{Hybrid Solver Rationale: ASP (Clingo) vs. Tabled Datalog (MulVAL)}
A key architectural choice in our pipeline is coupling Answer Set Programming with Horn-clause Datalog, leveraging Clingo and MulVAL for their respective strengths:

Clingo operates under the Closed World Assumption with stable model semantics, making it well-suited for non-monotonic reasoning, constraint satisfaction, and declarative entity completion. We utilize Clingo in System-2A to compute quadratic credential-sharing relations ($\text{sameCredentials}/2$), identify domain controller roles, and formalize unauthenticated protocol bindings under closed-world assumptions.

In contrast, MulVAL executes Horn-clause Datalog under monotonic logic, using goal-directed SLG resolution with tabling in XSB Prolog. This reasoning engine is optimized for polynomial-time transitive reachability queries across multi-hop firewall boundaries, constructing causal dependency graphs without being trapped by cyclic dependencies. Partitioning these tasks allows each declarative engine to handle the computational phase for which it is best suited.

\subsection{Theoretical Soundness \& Semantic Guardrails}
To ensure that stochastic inconsistencies from the language model cannot propagate into the symbolic reasoning engines, the pipeline enforces strict syntactic and semantic checks. 

The ingestion controller intercepts all raw text, normalizing strings to lowercase alphanumeric identifiers to avoid collisions with Prolog variable definitions. All generated atoms are validated against arity and type signatures, ensuring that malformed facts are discarded prior to ASP grounding. 

Finally, Clingo's grounding engine processes these atoms under stable model semantics, isolating any contradictory or ungrounded assertions. This ensures that only logically coherent, strictly typed facts are admitted into \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}, preserving the formal integrity of the downstream derivation engine.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation}
Let $\mathcal{C}_{JSON}$ represent the semi-structured industrial network configuration artifact describing the plant \cite{SunderOT}. The primary goal is to compile $\mathcal{C}_{JSON}$ into a formally sound, bipartite Logical Attack Graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{V} = \mathcal{V}_P \cup \mathcal{V}_D \cup \mathcal{V}_R
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{V}_P$ denotes primitive environmental facts extracted directly from asset configurations, $\mathcal{V}_D$ denotes derived vulnerability and privilege states, $\mathcal{V}_R$ denotes rule execution instances (AND-nodes), and $\mathcal{E}$ represents directed causal dependency transitions between these states \cite{Ou05}.

We formalize the overall transformation as the functional composition of three distinct mathematical operators:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G} = \mathcal{D}_{MulVAL}\Big(\mathcal{R}_{OT}, \; \mathcal{M}_{ASP}\big(\mathcal{K}_{KB}, \; \mathcal{T}_{LLM}(\mathcal{C}_{JSON}, \mathcal{P}_{EIN})\big)\Big)
\end{equation}
In this pipeline, $\mathcal{T}_{LLM}$ represents the System-1 neural extraction operator governed by prompt directives $\mathcal{P}_{EIN}$ to parse raw data into explicit facts. The operator $\mathcal{M}_{ASP}$ denotes the System-2A Answer Set Programming engine running in Clingo, which evaluates background knowledge rules $\mathcal{K}_{KB}$ under stable model semantics to infer implicit relations \cite{clingo}. Finally, $\mathcal{D}_{MulVAL}$ represents the System-2B Datalog engine running on XSB Prolog, which takes the materialized fact base and extended domain rules $\mathcal{R}_{OT} \equiv \texttt{attack1.P}$ to derive reachable attack paths \cite{Ou05}.

\label{sec:system1_implementation}

System-1 functions as an inductive parsing interface. Its operational role is restricted strictly to syntactic entity extraction, mapping key-value pairs from JSON subtrees into explicit relational Datalog atoms \cite{alviano2025llmasp}.

\subsection{Behavioral Framing and Domain Extraction Mappings}
To prevent generative drift and ensure consistent outputs, System-1 is controlled through two declarative configuration files:
\begin{enumerate}

\subsection{Payload Slicing and Token Optimization Dynamics}
A central practical problem identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis} is that passing complete production manifests into single prompts causes context saturation, model degradation, and API rate-limiting. To address this, the pipeline controller (\texttt{run\_llmasp.py}) implements dynamic \textit{payload slicing}.

Rather than sending the entire configuration file on every query, the controller uses \texttt{application\_ot.yaml} to slice out only the relevant subtree of $\mathcal{C}_{JSON}$ needed for each target predicate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item When extracting network reachability (\texttt{aclNW/4}), the prompt receives only the \texttt{firewall\_rules} array.
    \item When extracting Layer-2 fieldbus connections (\texttt{l2Connection/5}), only the \texttt{fieldbus\_connections} array is provided.
    \item When extracting telemetry paths (\texttt{dataFlow/4}), only the \texttt{data\_flows} block is passed.
    \item When extracting host-level properties (\texttt{located/3}, \texttt{networkService/5}, \texttt{hasAccount/3}, \texttt{vulHost/5}), the prompt receives a pruned version of the \texttt{hosts} array containing only the relevant attributes.
\end{itemize}
This targeted scoping reduces per-request prompt volume by up to 65\%. It keeps prompt sizes within API limits, prevents rate limiting, and improves extraction accuracy by focusing the model's attention entirely on the relevant schema fields.

\section{System-2: Declarative Symbolic Completion and Deductive Derivation}
\label{sec:system2_implementation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

System-2 is partitioned into two distinct declarative engines: Answer Set Programming in Clingo for background knowledge materialization (System-2A), and Datalog derivation in MulVAL for attack graph synthesis (System-2B) \cite{alviano2025llmasp}.

\subsection{System-2A: Declarative ASP Materialization via \textsc{Clingo}}
A key design choice of our methodology is offloading relational deduction and entity linking to Answer Set Programming \cite{clingo}. 

As identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, checking administrative credential sharing across a network requires pairwise comparisons over all host accounts, scaling quadratically as $O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)$. Asking a language model to find every matching pair across 21 machines often results in missed connections and hallucinated links. In our pipeline, the model extracts only the basic account facts (\texttt{hasAccount/3}). Deducing shared credentials is then handled symbolically by Clingo 5.x using background knowledge rules $\mathcal{K}_{KB}$ under stable model semantics:

\begin{equation}
    \text{sameCredentials}(H_1, H_2) \leftarrow \text{hasAccount}(H_1, U, P) \land \text{hasAccount}(H_2, U, P) \land H_1 \neq H_2
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{isDomainController}(H, \text{danieli\_domain}) \leftarrow \text{networkService}(H, \text{active\_directory}, \_, \_, \_)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{vulLinkProtocol}(H, \text{vul\_modbus\_no\_auth}, \text{modbus}, \text{adjacent}, \text{impersonateDst}) \leftarrow \text{networkService}(H, \text{modbus\_service}, \text{tcp}, 502, \_)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{isMaster}(H, \text{bus\_area\_1}) \leftarrow \text{networkService}(H, \text{modbus\_service}, \text{tcp}, 502, \_)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{isSlave}(D, \text{bus\_area\_1}) \leftarrow \text{located}(D, \text{fieldbus\_l0}, \_)
\end{equation}

This declarative step serves three specific functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Equation (5.3) calculates the symmetric transitive closure of credential sharing across all hosts. It identifies when different machines share administrative password hashes without requiring the language model to perform pairwise comparisons.
    \item Equation (5.4) identifies which hosts run Active Directory services and marks them as Domain Controllers, formalizing their role for domain-takeover rules.
    \item Equations (5.5) through (5.7) inspect active services and subnet locations to bind unauthenticated Modbus protocols to controllers and establish master-slave relationships over Layer-2 fieldbuses.
\end{itemize}
Clingo evaluates these rules in approximately 0.0060 seconds, ensuring that all relational links are derived quickly and without hallucinations \cite{clingo}. The resulting stable model is then combined with static axioms to produce the closed fact base: \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}.

\subsection{Vulnerability Modeling Mechanics: Static 5-Tuples vs. Dynamic NVD Ingestion}
In our framework, vulnerability data enters the pipeline as pre-classified 5-tuples:
\begin{equation}
    \text{vulHost}(Host, CVE\_Identifier, ServiceName, ExploitRange, Consequence)
\end{equation}

Within the symbolic Datalog engine, the $CVE\_Identifier$ parameter (e.g., \texttt{cve\_2022\_26809}) is semantically inert, serving strictly as an explanatory annotation label within attack graph nodes \cite{Ou05}. Deductive inference relies entirely on exact term unification of $ExploitRange$ (\texttt{remoteExploit}) and $Consequence$ (\texttt{privEscalation} vs. \texttt{dos}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item When $Consequence = \texttt{privEscalation}$, the fact is consumed by Rule~2 to derive arbitrary code execution (\texttt{execCode/2}).
    \item When $Consequence = \texttt{dos}$, the fact unifies exclusively with Rule~7, deriving control-loop denial of service (\texttt{dos/2}).
\end{itemize}

This decoupling of the vulnerability string from the logical consequence ensures that attack graph generation remains mathematically sound and independent of external API connectivity or database schema changes during evaluation \cite{Ou05}.

\section{Evaluation Methodology, Metrics, and Epistemological Ground Truth}
\label{sec:methodology_ground_truth}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To evaluate the pipeline rigorously, we must clarify the metrics used to measure performance and address how security verification is validated in industrial operational environments where classical ground truth datasets do not exist.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics Definition}
The performance, scalability, and quality of the pipeline are evaluated across five primary dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Syntactic Parsing Precision and Ingestion Latency ($T_{\text{neural}}$):} Measures the wall-clock execution time required by System-1 to parse targeted JSON subtrees into typed ground atoms, alongside the syntactic adherence of emitted atoms to the schema signatures defined in Table~\ref{tab:predicate_signatures}.
    \item \textbf{Declarative Completion Latency ($T_{\text{asp}}$):} Measures the computational duration required by Clingo 5.x to ground and solve the non-monotonic background rules ($\mathcal{K}_{KB}$), materializing implicit pairwise credential matches and protocol vulnerability bindings under stable model semantics.
    \item \textbf{Goal-Directed Derivation Time ($T_{\text{mulval}}$):} Measures the execution duration required by MulVAL running on XSB Prolog to resolve backward-chaining queries, construct the proof tree, and compile the Logical Attack Graph (LAG).
    \item \textbf{Derivation Complexity and Edge Density ($|\mathcal{E}|$):} Quantifies the structural size and density of the synthesized attack graph, tracking the number of logical dependency edges connecting AND derivation steps to OR state nodes.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual Collapse Rate:} Measures whether the derivation trees for target attack goals collapse from a satisfiable status (\textbf{DERIVABLE}) to an unsatisfiable status (\textbf{BLOCKED}) when specific firewall conduits or credential links are computationally revoked.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Ground Truth Dilemma in Operational Technology}
In conventional empirical computer science, such as natural language processing or image classification, systems are evaluated against an established ground truth test set using statistical metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-score \cite{WachterSurvey}. Similarly, in recent agentic web-security benchmarks (such as the CyBench evaluation conducted by Stevanovic and Wachter \cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}), ground truth is established by deploying Capture-The-Flag (CTF) challenges where the exact exploitable vulnerability sequence is documented in advance by the challenge authors \cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}.

In live industrial automation, however, this empirical paradigm cannot be replicated. An active steel production facility operating continuous casting machinery or rolling mills cannot be subjected to live cyberattacks, penetration testing exploits, or intentional denial-of-service disruptions to establish an empirical baseline of compromised states \cite{SunderOT, TayouriSurvey}. No physical manufacturing plant can provide historical, verified exploit logs detailing how real adversaries traversed its internal network layers.

Consequently, this research addresses an epistemological reality: \textbf{in OT threat modeling, ground truth cannot be an empirical trace of physical destruction}. Instead, ground truth is established through \textbf{Structural Configuration Fidelity} and \textbf{Formal Mathematical Soundness}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural Configuration Fidelity:} The ground truth is defined by the production engineering configuration artifact (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation. A pipeline extraction is accurate if and only if every emitted ground fact corresponds to an actual configuration property, without omitting declared firewall rules or inventing non-existent daemons.
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Soundness of Derivations:} Rather than measuring statistical probability, the validity of derived attack paths is governed by deductive mathematical proof. In Horn-clause Datalog, an attack goal $g$ is derived if and only if there exists a valid sequence of logical inferences from the axioms:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}_{\text{complete}} \cup \mathcal{R}_{OT} \vdash g
    \end{equation}
    Because System-2A and System-2B operate strictly as formal theorem provers, every edge in the synthesized attack graph represents a verified logical entailment. The concept of a hallucinated attack path is mathematically impossible within the symbolic layer; an attack path can only exist if permitted by the underlying configuration facts and interaction physics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Expectations vs. Empirical Derivations}
\label{sec:theoretical_vs_empirical}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Comparing the synthesized attack graphs with theoretical expectations exposes critical insights regarding industrial network design:

First, the empirical derivations expose a common discrepancy regarding the \textbf{Purdue Model hierarchy}. Standard theoretical models assume that an adversary must compromise Level~3 Operations and Level~2 Supervisory systems sequentially before reaching Level~1 controllers \cite{SunderOT}. In the Danieli configuration, however, an administrative maintenance exception (\texttt{aclNW(jump\_server, plc\_l1\_1, tcp, 502)}) bypasses supervisory layers entirely, allowing an attacker to compromise Level~1 controllers in only three logical hops from the Internet.

Second, the derivations demonstrate the \textbf{efficiency of exploitless lateral movement}. Across Scenarios 02, 03, and 06, once the perimeter DMZ is breached, full domain takeover and physical drive manipulation occur without exploiting any additional software vulnerabilities. Lateral propagation is driven entirely by credential reuse (\texttt{sameCredentials/2}) and unauthenticated field protocols (\texttt{vulLinkProtocol/5}). This proves that standard CVE patch management alone is insufficient for securing OT environments.

Third, the derivation engine maintained \textbf{consistent polynomial scalability}. By using XSB Prolog tabling, derivation latency remained stable across different target depths ($\approx 0.99$s to $1.37$s), even as graph complexity grew from 9 nodes in Scenario~01 to 22 nodes and 22 edges in Scenario~08 \cite{swift2012xsb}.

\subsection{Fulfilled Objectives and Validated Hypotheses}
The empirical results confirm that four core research objectives were comprehensively achieved:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Safe Out-of-Band Fact Extraction (Pillar 1 / Problem 1 \& 2):} The pipeline completely fulfilled the goal of extracting formal threat modeling primitives from raw industrial JSON manifests without generating live network traffic or active scanning probes \cite{Ou05, alviano2025llmasp}.     \item \textbf{Symbolic Relational Completion under Stable Models (Pillar 2 / Problem 3):} The integration of Clingo 5.x as System-2A resolved the quadratic combinatorial bottleneck ($O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)$) of cross-host credential matching. Clingo computed all pairwise administrative credential matches, classified Domain Controllers, and grounded fieldbus master-slave links in 0.0060 seconds without generative hallucinations, fulfilling the declarative completion objective.
    \item \textbf{Domain-Specific Modeling of Exploitless Vulnerabilities (Pillar 3 / Problem 4):} The pipeline successfully formalized Class~II industrial weaknesses within MulVAL's Datalog framework. By implementing Rules 5 through 9 in \texttt{attack1.P}, the derivation engine mathematically proved how unauthenticated Modbus/TCP command injection and Layer-2 PROFINET drive actuation cascade directly from compromised supervisory jump hosts without software memory-corruption exploits \cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.
    \item \textbf{Polynomial Scalability and Termination Guarantees (Problem 5):} Enforcing SLG resolution with XSB Prolog tabling directives (\texttt{:- table execCode/2.}) completely eliminated non-terminating loops caused by symmetric credential sharing and reciprocal firewall rules. Across all eight evaluated industrial threat scenarios, derivation times remained strictly bounded between 0.99 and 1.37 seconds, proving polynomial computational scaling ($O(N^2)$) on realistic plant topologies \cite{swift2012xsb}.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual Hardening Verification (Pillar 4):} The pipeline demonstrated predictive verification capabilities, proving that computationally revoking two high-priority structural exposures (the DMZ-to-PLC maintenance conduit and shared administrative credentials) collapsed the derivation trees for 6 out of the 8 threat scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Partially Fulfilled Objectives and Recommended Technical Approaches}
Two research objectives were only partially fulfilled by the current pipeline implementation, highlighting specific areas where further work is required:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Vendor Ingestion Generalization:} While System-1 automated extraction from Danieli's \texttt{configuration.json}, this mapping relied on tailored YAML schema extraction templates (\texttt{application\_ot.yaml}). The pipeline was not evaluated against differing industrial manifest formats, such as Siemens TIA Portal XML exports, AutomationML (IEC 62714) models, or Rockwell Automation inventories.
    \item \textbf{Suggested Approach for Cross-Vendor Generalization:} Future research should integrate an ontology alignment layer using Asset Administration Shell (AAS) metamodels (IEC 63278) or AutomationML parsers prior to System-1 ingestion. Rather than manually authoring YAML extraction mappings for each vendor, an intermediate semantic normalization pass could map heterogeneous vendor exports into a standardized ontological schema, allowing a single unified prompt manifest to govern System-1 across diverse industrial ecosystems.
    \item \textbf{Automated Domain Rule Synthesis from Cyber Threat Intelligence:} While entity extraction from the JSON manifest was automated, the extended Datalog interaction ruleset (\texttt{attack1.P}) was manually authored to model Purdue boundaries and OT protocols. The pipeline does not currently synthesize new Datalog interaction rules dynamically from unstructured threat intelligence feeds or vendor advisory bulletins (e.g., CISA ICS advisories) \cite{mitre_ics, StevanovicWachterRAISE}.
    \item \textbf{Suggested Approach for Automated Rule Synthesis:} To bridge this gap without introducing the severe path noise (83.9\%) observed in prior unconstrained studies \cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}, researchers should adopt a neuro-symbolic rule generation loop inspired by EntailLLM \cite{MukherjiEntailLLM}. A language model can be prompted to extract candidate precondition-action-postcondition triples from CISA advisories, which are then passed to Clingo for compile-time arity, type, and semantic validation before being merged into MulVAL's active rule base \cite{MukherjiEntailLLM}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope Boundaries and Opportunities for Extended Research}
\label{sec:scope_boundaries}

To delineate the analytical scope of this thesis and provide a foundation for subsequent investigations, several operational dimensions intentionally lie beyond our static, out-of-band threat modeling pipeline. These areas represent natural pathways for researchers and practitioners seeking to extend the pipeline's capabilities into broader operational domains.

\subsubsection{Dynamic and Non-Monotonic Defensive Adaptation}
The current pipeline operates under the monotonic logic paradigm, modeling accumulated adversarial reachability and persistent privileges to prove structural path satisfiability \cite{Ou05}. A valuable research direction involves extending the formal engine to account for dynamic, non-monotonic defensive interventions. Future work could integrate temporal logic models (e.g., LTL/CTL) or Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) \cite{WachterSurvey} to model time-varying defensive responses, such as automated session timeouts, rate-limiting thresholds, dynamic firewall pinhole expirations, and reactive Intrusion Detection System (IDS) TCP connection resets. Coupling these dynamic state transitions with our declarative knowledge base would bridge the gap between static path discovery and real-time operational response planning.

\subsubsection{Hardware-in-the-Loop and Physical Impact Validation}
Our formal derivation of \texttt{physicalDriveControl(jump\_server, drive\_area\_1\_1)} mathematically proves that unauthenticated Layer-2 PROFINET paths connect compromised supervisory controllers directly to physical variable frequency drives. Because live manufacturing machinery cannot be subjected to destructive testing, this analysis remains strictly topological and logical. An impactful progression for subsequent studies would be to deploy this pipeline against dedicated Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) testbeds or high-fidelity digital twins. Researchers can empirically measure whether forged PROFINET control frames successfully bypass hardware-level safety relays, local emergency stops, and physical interlocks, thereby quantifying the kinetic and mechanical consequences of derived attack trajectories under safe, isolated conditions.

\subsubsection{Automated Zero-Day and Latent Vulnerability Discovery}
The dual-class vulnerability engine is designed to assess known software flaws (Class~I CVEs) and architectural protocol exposures (Class~II exploitless vectors) declared within input manifests. A promising technical enhancement lies in combining the pipeline with dynamic fuzzing engines or symbolic binary execution tools. By ingesting discovered zero-day vulnerabilities and newly surfaced protocol anomalies directly into the System-1 extraction layer, researchers can evaluate how previously uncataloged vulnerabilities alter multi-stage lateral reachability across the Purdue hierarchy in an automated, closed-loop framework.

\section{Summary of Experimental Findings}
\label{sec:findings_summary}

The experimental evaluation confirms three overarching findings regarding automated industrial threat modeling:
First, the neuro-symbolic pipeline generated mathematically verified, zero-hallucination attack graphs across all eight targeted threat scenarios on the Danieli plant topology without missing intermediate lateral hops or failing arity constraints \cite{alviano2025llmasp}.
Second, the symbolic completion layer proved computationally scalable: Clingo executed background knowledge completion across quadratic credential relationships in 0.0060 seconds, while MulVAL compiled complete multi-stage attack graphs in an average of 1.12 seconds per scenario \cite{clingo, swift2012xsb}.
Finally, counterfactual verification confirmed that applying targeted architectural mitigations (blocking direct port 502 DMZ-to-PLC access and enforcing administrative credential segregation) broke the logical derivation trees for six out of the eight threat scenarios, demonstrating that formal declarative logic synthesis provides a practical, zero-risk methodology for industrial security hardening prior to physical plant commissioning \cite{SunderOT}.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis presented an end-to-end, neuro-symbolic framework for automated cyber-physical risk assessment in industrial Operational Technology (OT) environments, evaluated against the reference architecture of a Danieli automation plant. Industrial control networks present unique verification challenges due to their hybrid architectures, governed by the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture, where IT enterprise infrastructure directly converges with deterministic fieldbus protocols, programmable logic controllers (PLCs), and physical actuators. Traditional threat modeling methodologies rely heavily on manual rule authoring or static vulnerability scanning, both of which struggle to capture the complex, multi-stage causality spanning logical perimeter breaches down to Layer~0 physical actuation.

To address these limitations, our approach decoupled network threat modeling into a structured, three-tier neuro-symbolic pipeline. In the first stage, the neural extraction phase (System-1 via LLMASP) decomposed the ingestion task across seven discrete, prompt-sliced predicate definitions. An open Large Language Model extracted explicit topological primitives, firewall rules, credentials, software CVEs, and data flows directly from unstructured configuration inputs in 51.16 seconds without structural hallucination. Subsequently, the symbolic completion stage (System-2A via \textsc{Clingo}) grounded background domain constraints in 0.0060 seconds, computing transitive equivalence closures over shared credentials and fieldbus topologies to yield 90 ground Datalog facts without generative drift. In the final stage, formal attack graph synthesis (System-2B via MulVAL and XSB Prolog) executed domain-adapted industrial interaction rules using SLG resolution to synthesize sound, causal attack graphs.

The empirical evaluation across eight distinct operational attack scenarios, spanning initial DMZ ingress, transitive domain controller compromise, unauthenticated Modbus/TCP command injection, OPC UA denial-of-service, and telemetry falsification on the Level-2 historian, achieved 100\% derivation coverage. Derivation latencies remained under 1.4 seconds per scenario, confirming that formal, causal safety guarantees can be derived deterministically from automated network extractions. The resulting pipeline was fully containerized and instrumented with deterministic replay mechanisms to guarantee academic reproducibility.

\section{Future Works}
\label{sec:future_works}

While the current framework demonstrates the validity and formal soundness of neuro-symbolic attack graph generation, several avenues remain for extension.

A primary direction concerns fine-grained lateral transport modeling. The current credential-reuse interaction rules assume administrative reachability whenever identical credential hashes are discovered across hosts. Future work should couple credential propagation rules with strict network segmentation predicates, such as verifying explicit transport conduits for RDP, WinRM, SSH, or SMB across Purdue levels, to capture micro-segmentation policies more faithfully.

Another critical extension involves multi-vector ingress analysis. The present reference evaluation models initial perimeter exposure primarily through an exposed, vulnerable jump server. Extending the input topology to incorporate alternate ingress surfaces, such as remote-access VPN endpoints, compromised engineering laptops, or supply-chain update channels, will enable comparative resilience scoring under diversified threat models.

Furthermore, dynamic risk metrics and probabilistic scoring represent a valuable enhancement. Although the derived graphs represent complete causal dependency trees, the evaluation currently treats exploitability as binary. Integrating dynamic probabilistic metrics, such as CVSS Exploitability sub-scores, the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS), and OT-specific operational disruption costs, into the MulVAL reasoning engine would enable quantitative risk prioritization and optimal remediation path identification.

Finally, the framework can be extended toward closed-loop automated hardening and prescriptive defense. While our counterfactual analysis demonstrated that eliminating perimeter vulnerabilities mitigates all downstream paths, future iterations could integrate automated synthesis of minimal-cut remediation strategies. Formulating countermeasure selection as an Answer Set Optimization or MaxSAT problem would allow the framework to prescribe the minimum set of firewall updates or host patches required to sever all viable paths to Layer~0 assets.
