\begin{abstract}
As operational technology (OT) environments increasingly converge with corporate enterprise networks, ensuring cybersecurity without disrupting critical industrial processes has become a central challenge. Traditional IT active vulnerability scanning techniques, such as broad port sweeps and aggressive probing, cannot be safely deployed on fragile industrial automation assets like Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) and fieldbus drives without risking buffer exhaustion, control-loop blindness, or physical downtime. Consequently, at the moment, security assessment in operational technology must still be conducted entirely out-of-band using formal modeling over static asset inventories.

This thesis explores \textit{automating} threat modeling in OT using a neuro-symbolic pipeline designed to bridge the gap between semi-structured industrial configuration manifests and formal declarative logic engines. The framework combines inductive neural parsing via the LLMASP methodology, Answer Set Programming (Clingo) for declarative knowledge completion, and tabled Horn-clause logic derivation (MulVAL and XSB Prolog) to synthesize multi-stage attack paths from a pre-defined, custom rule base, developed as part of this thesis, without generating live network traffic. Evaluated against a realistic plant configuration model provided by Danieli Automation, the pipeline demonstrates how combining neural extraction with symbolic theorem proving enables structured, non-disruptive threat analysis, and supports counterfactual security hardening against both classical software vulnerabilities and exploitless operational protocol flaws.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Industrial Paradigm Shift \& Contextualizing OT Constraints}
Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Operational Technology (OT) networks form the critical infrastructure backbone of contemporary manufacturing plants, power generation facilities, water treatment utilities, and heavy industrial operations~\cite{SunderOT}. Historically, these architectures were physically isolated from external communications through dedicated air-gaps, relying on specialized hardware and vendor-proprietary, closed-loop protocols~\cite{StanExtended}. However, the rapid acceleration of Industry 4.0 paradigms, Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) platforms, and centralized manufacturing telemetry has forced a widespread structural convergence between corporate Information Technology (IT) enterprise networks and operational production environments.

While this interconnectivity facilitates real-time supply-chain analytics and automated resource management, directly porting enterprise IT security frameworks into operational technology ecosystems introduces profound systemic friction due to fundamentally opposing operational priorities. In enterprise IT ecosystems, cybersecurity architectures are governed by the classical CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability), wherein data-at-rest encryption, data-in-transit privacy, and access control policies take precedence over operational continuity. 

In OT environments, availability, reliability, and safety often receive greater operational priority, while confidentiality and integrity remain important security objectives~\cite{SunderOT}. While unauthorized data exposure within an enterprise IT environment could incur regulatory penalties or financial liabilities, an unplanned shutdown, delayed control loop, or buffer fault in an industrial automation plant could precipitate catastrophic kinetic failures, physical equipment destruction, environmental disasters, and fatal occupational hazards. As a result, standard enterprise strategies, such as routine intrusive vulnerability scanning, automated operating system patching, and reactive fail-closed network terminations, cannot be safely executed on active production lines.

\section{The Evolving Threat Landscape and Operational Urgency in OT}
\label{sec:ot_urgency}
In recent years, the frequency of security advisories and reported vulnerabilities targeting Operational Technology has expanded significantly, reflecting a substantial shift in the adversarial landscape~\cite{MITRE_ICS_2024}. While early industrial security incidents were often regarded as rare, highly specialized operations conducted exclusively by advanced nation-state actors, such as the seminal Stuxnet worm~\cite{langner2011stuxnet}, the 2015/2016 Ukrainian power grid disruptions via Industroyer~\cite{case2016ukraine}, and the targeted safety instrumented system malware TRITON/HatMan~\cite{johnson2017triton}, contemporary industrial environments confront a much broader and more commoditized spectrum of risk. Industrial cybersecurity monitoring entities, emergency readiness teams such as CISA, and major automation vendors regularly disclose weaknesses across the industrial stack, ranging from supervisory software suites and engineering workstations down to embedded controller firmware and communication stacks~\cite{mitre_ics}. While early industrial security incidents were often regarded as rare, highly specialized operations conducted exclusively by advanced nation-state actors~\cite{langner2011stuxnet, case2016ukraine}, contemporary industrial environments confront a much broader and more commoditized spectrum of risk.

Targeted industrial ransomware has emerged as a prominent manifestation of this development, where adversaries no longer confine their campaigns to corporate business tiers but deliberately seek out operational jump hosts, supervisory storage repositories, and central backup systems to halt physical production lines and maximize extortion leverage~\cite{SunderOT}. In parallel, common border infrastructure and administrative conduits, such as remote desktop gateways, VPN endpoints, and remote procedure call services operating within the industrial DMZ, are regularly targeted by newly cataloged remote code execution flaws, providing adversaries with straightforward footholds into the automation perimeter~\cite{Ou05, TayouriSurvey}. 

Compounding these perimeter risks is the prevalence of native controller and protocol weaknesses that persist within the internal automation architecture~\cite{StanExtended}. Beyond traditional memory corruption vulnerabilities in software binaries, security advisories continually document systemic exposures stemming from legacy fieldbus communications, where protocols like Modbus/TCP or standard PROFINET operate without cryptographic session authentication or integrity verification by design~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. Once network reachability is established, an adversary can manipulate operational parameters, forge command frames, or induce denial-of-service states against embedded controllers without needing to exploit complex software bugs~\cite{StanExtended}.

The operational urgency to address these exposures stems from the structural dilemma of industrial lifecycles. Heavy manufacturing facilities operate with equipment intended to function reliably for decades, meaning that facilities inherently accumulate technical debt: legacy controllers originally deployed under the assumption of physical isolation are now directly interconnected with supervisory networks and remote maintenance links to satisfy modern data demands. Because these production devices cannot be easily patched, continually upgraded, or actively scanned without introducing severe risks of downtime, plant engineers face an urgent operational requirement for systematic, non-invasive assessment techniques to understand architectural exposure before adversaries can exploit these paths~\cite{SunderOT}. Within this context, this thesis does not claim to present an exhaustive, universal solution to industrial cyber risk, but rather proposes an automated, declarative threat modeling pipeline as a conceptual step forward to help practitioners map multi-stage attack paths safely out-of-band~\cite{Ou05, alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{The Economic and Physical Risks of Active Vulnerability Testing}
Because industrial automation assets, such as Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs), Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), and Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) servers, frequently run legacy embedded software stacks, they exhibit acute structural fragility~\cite{SunderOT}. In enterprise IT risk management, practitioners rely on active network scanners (e.g., Nessus, OpenVAS, aggressive \texttt{nmap} sweeps) to probe transport ports, transmit malformed request packets, and extract service banners to discover software Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs)~\cite{Ou05}.

Within an operational technology environment, however, the high volume of concurrent socket connections, non-standard payload formats, and burst packet rates generated by these active scanners can instantly overwhelm the single-threaded compute engines and finite memory buffers of embedded field devices. This buffer exhaustion can force PLCs into fatal hardware fault modes, disrupt cycle times, or induce control-loop blindness in supervisory SCADA systems. 

Given that unplanned downtime in capital-intensive heavy industries (such as automated steel manufacturing, chemical refining, and automotive production) incurs severe financial penalties and physical safety hazards, standard active scanning practices cannot be routinely conducted on live production lines~\cite{tayouri2023survey}. While industrial cybersecurity frameworks such as NIST SP~800-82 do not prohibit active penetration testing entirely, they mandate extreme caution: active probing can destabilize sensitive OT devices or disrupt deterministic process states, requiring such assessments to be restricted, where feasible, to scheduled maintenance outages. 

However, heavy manufacturing facilities operate continuously under multi-decade lifecycles, meaning that fully isolated maintenance windows are infrequent and operationally costly. Relying exclusively on intermittent runtime testing leaves substantial temporal gaps in plant security posture. Consequently, this creates a compelling operational need for complementary, entirely out-of-band threat modeling methodologies that evaluate architectural risk over static configuration manifests and asset inventories without inducing physical disruption.

\section{Limitations of Manual Risk Analysis in Large-Scale Industrial Automation}

Compounding the challenge of non-intrusive assessment is the sheer scale and structural complexity of modern industrial facilities~\cite{Sunder2024}. Partitioned across hierarchical operational tiers under the ANSI/ISA-95 Purdue Reference Model, contemporary plants encompass deeply interconnected architectures where supervisory servers, identity management services, virtualized workstations, and hundreds of embedded controllers continuously interact across strict perimeter conduits~\cite{Ou05, Stan2023}. 

Evaluating the security posture of such multi-tiered environments through manual risk assessment is inherently inefficient, non-exhaustive, and prone to human oversight~\cite{Konsta2024}. Human auditors routinely evaluate assets or subnets in isolation, struggling to grasp the combinatorial explosion of potential multi-hop propagation paths that emerge from overlapping firewall exceptions, cross-zone routing conduits, and pervasive administrative credential reuse.

To address these cognitive and scalability bottlenecks, the automated synthesis of Logical Attack Graphs (LAGs) represents a particularly promising and effective approach~\cite{Ou05, Tayouri2026}. By synthesizing network topologies, access control policies, host configurations, and industrial protocol properties into formal declarative logic, automated reasoning engines can mathematically explore the combinatorial state space, deriving whether an external adversary can pivot across intermediate supervisory tiers to compromise critical physical processes~\cite{Ou05, Sunder2024}.

\section{Objectives and Scope of the Thesis Research}
\label{sec:objectives}
\label{sec:objectives_scope}

Given the operational risks of runtime probing on legacy industrial devices and the prevalence of exploitless propagation vectors across segmented architectures, conducting threat modeling directly from static asset inventories presents substantial practical appeal. However, bridging the gap between semi-structured configuration manifests and formal derivation engines introduces distinct representational, combinatorial, and domain-specific challenges. 

Motivated by these operational realities, the core problem addressed by this thesis is formulated around the following central research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{To what extent can a schema-constrained neuro-symbolic framework synthesize verifiable attack graphs from static OT manifests, enabling non-disruptive threat analysis and mitigation assessment?}
\end{quote}

Rather than asserting an exhaustive solution across all proprietary automation architectures, this research investigates the feasibility and boundaries of this approach on a realistic plant configuration model provided during an internship at Danieli Automation. To answer the central research question, the technical contributions of this thesis are structured across four specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Neuro-Symbolic Fact Ingestion on an Industrial Case Study:} Investigating a structured division of labor using the LLMASP framework applied to a specific industrial schema. Constraining an LLM strictly to inductive syntactic extraction---guided by targeted payload slicing and plant-specific schema mappings---to reliably parse semi-structured configuration manifests into typed Datalog atoms without generative hallucinations or syntax collisions.
    \item \textbf{Declarative Knowledge Completion:} Implementing an Answer Set Programming (ASP) component using Clingo to compute implicit structural properties under stable model semantics, including the quadratic equivalence closure of shared administrative credentials and unauthenticated fieldbus bindings, before submitting facts to the derivation engine.
    \item \textbf{OT-Specific Logic Formulation:} Extending the MulVAL Datalog reasoning engine with domain-specific Horn-clause interaction rules that natively capture Purdue Model boundaries, credential reuse, unauthenticated Modbus/TCP command injection, and Layer-2 PROFINET drive manipulation using tabled resolution for polynomial execution.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation and Counterfactual Hardening:} Benchmarking the end-to-end pipeline across representative attack scenarios targeting supervisory services, Level~1 controllers, and physical field drives within the Danieli network model, and evaluating whether derived attack graphs deterministically collapse when specific firewall conduits and credential links are computationally revoked.
\end{enumerate}
Through this approach, this thesis demonstrates how combining neural semantic extraction with formal declarative solvers can support non-disruptive threat analysis and security planning for a specific, realistic industrial topology without endangering physical plant operations.

\section{Security Paradigms: IT vs. OT}
\label{sec:it_vs_ot}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Understanding the vulnerabilities of cyber-physical systems (CPS) requires analyzing the structural divergence between traditional IT and OT ecosystems~\cite{SunderOT, StanExtended}. While modern Industry 4.0 initiatives drive interconnectivity between these spaces, their engineering designs, failure modes, and security objectives remain fundamentally distinct.

In enterprise IT systems, cybersecurity architectures are governed by the classical CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity, and availability). Data privacy, access authorization, and cryptographic isolation take precedence. IT environments are characterized by relatively homogeneous computing platforms, regular software patching schedules, standardized operating systems, and high tolerance for transient reboots or dynamic connection resets. Security assessments in IT routinely leverage automated, intrusive network scanners, such as Nessus, OpenVAS, or aggressive Nmap service sweeps (e.g., executing broad \texttt{nmap -sV} commands), to transmit thousands of malformed probe packets, identify open transport sockets, and extract software release banners~\cite{Ou05}.

Within operational technology, this priority hierarchy undergoes an inversion, prioritizing availability, deterministic timing, and physical safety over confidentiality~\cite{SunderOT}. 

OT encompasses supervisory architectures and distributed embedded controllers governing real-world physical dynamics: chemical reactions, high-voltage power routing, and automated steel manufacturing lines~\cite{StanExtended}. Unlike enterprise servers, embedded Level~1 programmable logic controllers (PLCs), remote terminal units (RTUs), and Level~0 variable frequency drives feature single-threaded microcontrollers, fixed cyclic execution loops, and constrained network buffers.

Subjecting these embedded industrial devices to active IT scanning methodologies precipitates severe physical risks. The high connection volume and packet frequencies generated by standard vulnerability scanners can exhaust controller communication buffers, forcing PLCs into non-recoverable hardware fault states, disrupting millisecond-level scan cycles, or blinding human-machine interfaces (HMIs). Because unexpected downtime in capital-intensive manufacturing plants incurs severe financial loss and physical safety hazards, active scanning and intrusive penetration testing are strictly excluded from operational networks~\cite{TayouriSurvey}. Security evaluation in OT must therefore be conducted entirely out-of-band through formal, declarative modeling over static configuration manifests and asset inventories~\cite{Ou05, TayouriMIRAGE, alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{Hierarchical Industrial Network Architectures: Purdue and ISA-95}
\label{sec:purdue_model}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To prevent disruptions to deterministic automation equipment caused by traffic or threats from corporate business networks, industrial facilities are organized according to hierarchical architectural models. Historically, this partitioning originates from the \textit{Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture} (PERA) developed in the 1990s as a functional control model, later standardized by ANSI/ISA-95 to define the integration between manufacturing operations and enterprise business logistics~\cite{isa95}. 

While ISA-95 and classic PERA define operational functionality across Levels~0 through~4, modern industrial cybersecurity standards---notably IEC~62443 and NIST SP~800-82---adapt this hierarchy into defensive \textit{zones and conduits}~\cite{stouffer2023nist}. Within this security adaptation, an intermediate boundary designated as \textbf{Level~3.5 (Industrial DMZ)} is introduced as a necessary security buffer to terminate direct layer-to-layer sessions between enterprise networks and the factory floor. 

The resulting reference hierarchy, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:purdue_model_overview}, spans from external corporate operations down to physical actuation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level 5 / Enterprise Network (Corporate/WAN):} Encompasses multi-site corporate networks, centralized cloud services, and wide-area business infrastructure outside the local plant boundary.
    \item \textbf{Level 4 / Business Logistics and Enterprise Systems:} Hosts site-level business management systems, including Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), supply chain tracking, and local corporate database services.
    \item \textbf{Level 3.5 / Industrial Demilitarized Zone (IDMZ):} A specialized security demarcation layer recommended by IEC~62443 and NIST SP~800-82. It hosts jump servers, patch management proxies, and secondary historians, enforcing the termination of direct sessions between business IT and industrial operational tiers.
    \item \textbf{Level 3 / Site Operations Management:} Houses plant-wide supervisory control infrastructure, continuous process historians (e.g., ibaHD), engineering workstations (EWS), and operational identity providers (such as plant-specific Active Directory domain controllers).
    \item \textbf{Level 2 / Supervisory Control:} Encompasses local Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs), supervisory SCADA control rooms, and distributed automation management terminals governing localized manufacturing cells.
    \item \textbf{Level 1 / Basic and Continuous Control:} Contains Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), Remote Terminal Units (RTUs), and safety instrumented controllers running hard real-time cyclic control loops.
    \item \textbf{Level 0 / Physical Process and Field Equipment:} Encompasses physical field instrumentation, variable frequency drives (VFDs), actuators, motors, pneumatic valves, and sensors interacting directly with the physical manufacturing medium.
\end{itemize}

Traditional enterprise vulnerability assessment tools frequently evaluate topologies as flat, undifferentiated network graphs~\cite{Ou05}. To construct a mathematically sound and domain-accurate attack model, reasoning engines cannot treat these zones uniformly; they must enforce these strict Purdue boundaries and cross-level conduit constraints using explicit structural predicates.

\section{Foundations of Logic-Based Attack Graph Analysis}
\label{sec:logic_foundations}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Automating security analysis over multi-tiered networks requires formal representations capable of modeling causal relationships across disparate vulnerabilities, access control tokens, and privilege states. This section reviews the generation paradigms for attack graphs and formalizes the transition from combinatorial state-space exploration to monotonic logic deduction.

\subsection{Attack Graph Generation Paradigms}
\label{sec:ag_paradigms}
In comprehensive methodological surveys of attack graph formalisms, security researchers categorize automated attack modeling frameworks into three primary generation paradigms~\cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pure Graph-Theoretic Architectures:} These systems construct explicit directed graphs to map paths, relying on classical graph traversal algorithms (e.g., Dijkstra, Breadth-First Search) to evaluate reachability. While conceptually intuitive and effective for small networks, they remain constrained by state-space explosion when applied to complex topologies.
    \item \textbf{Model-Checking Frameworks:} Leveraging formal verification engines such as NuSMV or SPIN, these approaches model network components as strict finite state machines and use temporal logic formulas (e.g., LTL or CTL) to verify safety properties. While mathematically rigorous and capable of expressing non-monotonic state revocations, they demand substantial memory and computational time in large infrastructures.
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Declarative Logic Engines:} Frameworks such as MulVAL leverage Horn-clause logic programming and backward-chaining engines to evaluate security risks~\cite{Ou05}. This architecture provides scalability and expressiveness by abstracting network configurations into static relational facts and generic interaction rules, natively maintaining a monotonic view of the environment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From State-Space Models to Horn-Clause Reasoning}
\label{sec:state_space_to_horn}
The early formalization of automated security analysis in networked environments relied on state-space transition systems~\cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}. In these models, security analysis was treated as a verification problem where the entire network infrastructure was modeled as a global finite state machine. Each node in the graph represented an explicit snapshot of the network, capturing the operating system state, patch levels, open ports, and active privilege levels across every host simultaneously. Edges between these state nodes represented discrete atomic state transitions, such as an adversary executing an exploit to transition the network from one global configuration state to another.

While conceptually straightforward, state-space transition systems suffer from a severe computational limitation known as \textit{state-space explosion}~\cite{Ou05, WachterSurvey}. Because these frameworks attempt to track all global state permutations, the size of the state graph scales exponentially with the number of networked assets and associated vulnerabilities. For a network containing $H$ distinct hosts, where each host can occupy one of $S$ distinct security configurations, the theoretical state space expands to:
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{S}| = O(S^H)
\end{equation}

When applied to modern industrial automation infrastructures, which routinely comprise dozens of virtualized supervisory nodes, distributed HMIs, engineering workstations, and embedded field controllers, computing the complete state transition graph quickly exceeds memory limits~\cite{KonstaSurvey, WachterSurvey}. This computational bottleneck renders dynamic risk assessment mathematically unfeasible under a classical state-space architecture.

To bypass the exponential overhead of state-space exploration, modern predictive security modeling transitioned to a formal logic programming paradigm~\cite{Ou05, WachterSurvey}. Pioneered by frameworks like MulVAL (Multi-host, Multi-stage Vulnerability Analysis Language), this methodology reformulates threat analysis from state-space searching into declarative theorem proving. Rather than tracking global system states, a logic-based model represents the network through declarative clauses expressed in a subset of first-order logic known as Horn-clause Datalog.

\paragraph{Datalog Notation and Inference.} MulVAL represents system properties and attacker capabilities using logical atoms. An atom consists of a predicate name and its arguments, such as:
\begin{center}
\texttt{networkService(plc1, modbus, tcp, 502, user)}
\end{center}
The predicate identifies a relation, while its arguments identify the entities or values participating in that relation. In the Prolog-style notation used by MulVAL, variables begin with uppercase letters or an underscore (\texttt{\_}), whereas symbolic constants generally begin with lowercase letters or appear in single quotation marks. An atom containing no variables is called a \textit{ground atom}.

Initial facts are ground atoms supplied as inputs to the analysis, describing the modeled environment and initial attacker capabilities. Derived facts are ground atoms inferred from these inputs through interaction rules. For example, an initial fact may describe a service running on a host, whereas a derived fact may establish that an attacker can execute code on that host.

Rules have the form \texttt{Head :- Body.}, where \texttt{:-} means ``if'' and corresponds to the left arrow ($\leftarrow$) in mathematical notation. Commas in the rule body express logical conjunction: every condition in the body must hold under a consistent assignment of values to variables for the head to be inferred. Commas inside an atom instead separate its arguments. A period terminates a fact or rule. Thus, the rule:
\begin{center}
\texttt{p(X) :- q(X), r(X).}
\end{center}
states that \texttt{p(X)} follows whenever both \texttt{q(X)} and \texttt{r(X)} hold for the same value of \texttt{X}.

Mathematically, a Horn clause is a disjunction of literals containing at most one positive literal, written in rule format as:
\begin{equation}
H \leftarrow B_1 \land B_2 \land \dots \land B_m
\end{equation}
where the head $H$ is derived as a logical consequence if all body subgoals $B_1, \dots, B_m$ evaluate to true~\cite{Ou05}.

\paragraph{Monotonicity Guarantees.} This paradigm shift relies fundamentally on the principle of \textit{monotonicity}~\cite{Ou05}. In a monotonic logic framework, it is assumed that an adversary's acquired privileges and network reachability accumulate over time and are never revoked during the evaluated attack sequence. If an attacker acquires administrative control over a jump server, that privilege remains a true logical fact throughout the duration of the multi-stage evaluation.

Importantly, this monotonicity guarantee applies to MulVAL's core interaction rules and our extended industrial rules, which utilize positive Datalog: facts, rule heads, and rule bodies contain exclusively positive atoms. For a fixed rule set, adding facts therefore strictly preserves previously derived conclusions. While the underlying XSB Prolog engine supports negation as failure, which can introduce non-monotonic behaviors, restricting domain rules to positive Horn clauses guarantees monotonic derivation. Consequently, the computational complexity of attack path synthesis is drastically reduced from exponential time to polynomial time $O(N^2)$, where $N$ represents the total volume of ground configuration facts, reachability predicates, and vulnerability assertions in the knowledge base~\cite{Ou05}.

\subsection{Path Discovery and Optimization}
\label{sec:path_discovery_opt}
As network infrastructures expand to encompass hundreds of interconnected enterprise and operational assets, the total volume of ground configuration facts grows proportionally~\cite{AbdellatifAAPDEM}. While monotonic Horn-clause Datalog solvers operate in polynomial time $O(N^2)$, evaluating thousands of rules and predicates across complex corporate-to-OT boundaries can yield extensive derivation trees.

To address computational overhead in large topologies, recent literature has explored specialized path optimization heuristics, including cost-aware $A^*$ search algorithms (such as the AAPDEM model~\cite{AbdellatifAAPDEM}) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) pathfinding agents~\cite{WachterSurvey}. While these learning-based models provide value for rapid path pruning in hyper-scale cloud environments, they introduce trade-offs in verification explainability and completeness.

In safety-critical industrial automation plants, exhaustive completeness and deterministic formal soundness are mandatory requirements~\cite{SunderOT}. Because industrial plant topologies (comprising dozens of servers, engineering workstations, and Level 1 PLCs) remain well within the polynomial scaling envelope of Datalog engines, this research adopts exhaustive, goal-directed backward-chaining powered by XSB Prolog tabling (memoization)~\cite{swift2012xsb}. Tabling eliminates redundant re-evaluations and guarantees that all possible adversarial propagation routes are identified deterministically.

\section{MulVAL and OT Domain-Specific Extensions}
\label{sec:mulval_domain_extensions}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Building on monotonic Horn-clause logic, this section examines the practical implementation of attack graph synthesis in MulVAL and details the domain extensions necessary to capture operational technology constraints.

\subsection{Engine Architecture, Configuration Facts, and Interaction Rules}
\label{sec:mulval_arch_components}
The core execution architecture of MulVAL achieves high scalability by separating the threat model into two distinct, decoupled components: static configuration facts and generic interaction rules~\cite{Ou05}.

\paragraph{Static Configuration Facts.} These represent the immutable, factual configuration of the target network environment~\cite{Ou05}. They are expressed as ground Datalog clauses registering network topology, firewall access control lists (ACLs), software installations, and software vulnerabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{networkService(Host, Program, Protocol, Port, Privilege)}: Defines a listening service binding, port number, and its local execution permissions.
    \item \texttt{vulHost(Host, CVE, Program, ExploitType, Consequence)}: Binds vulnerability intelligence and exploitation consequences to specific hosts.
    \item \texttt{aclNW(Source, Destination, Protocol, Port)}: Represents network filtering, routing reachability, and firewall policies.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Generic Interaction Rules.} These rules act as the underlying mathematical ``adversarial physics'' that dictate how an attacker can propagate through an environment~\cite{Ou05}. These are written as abstracted Horn clauses that define the conditions under which a new logical deduction can be derived from existing facts. For example, a standard remote software exploit interaction rule is formalized as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{execCode}(\text{Target}, \text{Priv}) \leftarrow \; & \text{networkService}(\text{Target}, \text{Prog}, \text{Prot}, \text{Port}, \text{Priv}), \\
& \text{vulHost}(\text{Target}, \text{CVE}, \text{Prog}, \text{remoteExploit}, \text{privEscalation}), \\
& \text{netAccess}(\text{Principal}, \text{AttackerHost}, \text{Target}, \text{Prot}, \text{Port}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Goal-Directed Resolution and XSB Tabling}
\label{sec:xsb_tabling_mechanics}
MulVAL utilizes an underlying XSB Prolog execution engine to perform goal-directed, backward-chaining derivation~\cite{swift2012xsb}. Given a user-defined target condition, such as:
\begin{equation}
\texttt{attackGoal(unauthorizedControl(plc\_l1\_1))}
\end{equation}
the engine resolves backward, evaluating which combinations of interaction rules and configuration facts can satisfy the terminal objective.

To optimize this process and prevent non-terminating loops caused by cyclic network dependencies (such as two hosts running identical software and sharing reciprocal network access), the engine leverages XSB Tabling Optimization Directives (e.g., \texttt{:- table execCode/2.})~\cite{swift2012xsb}. Tabling enforces memoization: once a specific security subgoal is evaluated, its truth value is cached in an internal lookup table. If the engine encounters the same subgoal later in another branch of the attack tree, it retrieves the cached result instantly rather than re-evaluating the entire logic sequence. This ensures that the engine dynamically constructs a comprehensive Logical Attack Graph (LAG) while maintaining low polynomial computation times~\cite{Ou05}.

\subsection{Limitations of IT-Centric Logic in Industrial Environments}
\label{sec:mulval_it_limitations}
While foundational logic engines such as MulVAL revolutionized automated threat modeling, their standard rule sets are inherently constrained to traditional IT abstractions~\cite{Ou05, StanExtended}. In a standard IT security model, system interactions are generalized around standard operating system permissions (e.g., \texttt{user}, \texttt{admin}, \texttt{root}), network connections established over standard TCP/IP sockets, and software vulnerabilities cataloged exclusively via the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) index.

Standard vulnerability scanners and IT-centric logic engines exhibit three primary blind spots when analyzing industrial network configurations~\cite{SunderOT}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Individual Asset Assessment:} They evaluate assets in isolation, assuming that the absence of a known software CVE equates to a secure operational state~\cite{Ou05}.
    \item \textbf{Flat Topology Assumptions:} Traditional logic engines treat network filtering policies as simple end-to-end reachability checks, failing to capture the cascading consequences that zone-to-zone firewall policies impose across multi-tier Purdue layers~\cite{SunderOT}.
    \item \textbf{Protocol Blindness:} Traditional IT logic structures lack predicates to express the unauthenticated and unencrypted landscape of legacy industrial communications pervasive across lower architectural layers~\cite{StanExtended}. In these networks, compromise does not merely manifest as user-to-root privilege escalation; instead, it frequently involves the unauthorized injection of unauthenticated control commands directly into a physical process loop.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Industrial Extensions: Segmentation, Protocol Flaws, and Credential Reuse}
\label{sec:industrial_logic_extensions}
To capture realistic multi-stage adversarial pathways within industrial infrastructures, formal logic threat models must be extended with domain-specific interaction rules that natively capture network hierarchies, industrial protocol behaviors, and cyber-physical dependencies~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. In our framework, these domain-specific extensions are organized across four structural tiers:

\paragraph{1. Purdue Boundary Placement Facts.}
Traditional logic engines treat network topologies as flat structures. To build a mathematically precise model of an industrial facility, the Datalog engine must enforce Purdue boundaries using explicit structural facts~\cite{SunderOT}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
located(jump_server, ot_dmz, ipSubnet).
located(vdc01, enterprise_mgt, ipSubnet).
located(ews_workstation, level_2_supervisory, ipSubnet).
located(plc_l1_1, level_1_control, ipSubnet).
located(drive_area_1_1, level_0_fieldbus, l2Bus).
\end{lstlisting}
By explicitly declaring these operational boundaries, the reasoning engine enforces perimeter-crossing constraints, modeling how an adversary positioned at an external perimeter must pivot through intermediate supervisory zones before reaching critical Level~1 control assets~\cite{SunderOT}.

\paragraph{2. Firewall Permissions and Zone-to-Zone Access.}
Because network segmentation via industrial firewalls represents the primary line of defense in an OT infrastructure, the logic model enforces a strict network filtering matrix~\cite{SunderOT}. In an industrial plant, network access is constrained: assets in a Level~2 supervisory network are restricted from interacting directly with Level~0/1 field equipment, except through explicitly authorized, tightly restricted firewall conduits (e.g., an HMI client polling a specific PLC over a dedicated industrial port). Extended Datalog models represent this structural access control matrix using explicit network access tokens (\texttt{aclNW/4}), defining permissible paths through firewalls~\cite{Ou05, SunderOT}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
aclNW(jump_server, vdc01, tcp, 445).
aclNW(jump_server, plc_l1_1, tcp, 502).
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{3. Unauthenticated Industrial Protocols and Exploitless Flaws.}
A critical operational risk in industrial automation stems from the pervasive deployment of standard, unhardened implementations of industrial protocols such as baseline Modbus/TCP, classic PROFINET, and EtherNet/IP~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. While modernized security extensions exist---such as the Modbus Security specification (which encapsulates traffic over TLS using X.509 certificates on TCP port 802) and PROFINET Security Classes (providing cryptographic integrity checks)---legacy installations and brownfield deployments predominantly rely on standard, cleartext implementations operating without cryptographic session authentication, packet integrity checks, or encryption~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}. 

In such unauthenticated baseline configurations, establishing network reachability to an exposed controller port significantly expands the adversary's attack surface. Where controllers operate without additional application-layer access controls, memory-protection boundaries, or physical hardware write-protect keyswitches, an attacker capable of routing packets to the service can issue unauthorized operational function codes directly. This enables exploitless threat vectors, such as spoofing control commands, altering process setpoints, or manipulating device state registers, without requiring software memory-corruption exploits (CVEs)~\cite{StanExtended}.

To formally capture these protocol-level characteristics within the declarative reasoning engine, the threat model incorporates domain-specific interaction rules that represent vulnerabilities inherent to unhardened communication channels rather than software implementation flaws:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
vulLinkProtocol(sub_proc_area_1, vul_modbus_no_auth, modbus, adjacent, impersonateDst).
\end{lstlisting}
These custom interaction rules were formulated through a human-in-the-loop engineering methodology. The protocol weaknesses and exploitation mechanics represented in these rules were researched and selected by the author based on operational discussions with plant engineers at Danieli Automation, academic literature reviews, and the structural constraints of the Purdue model.

When an adversary achieves network reachability to an unauthenticated controller port, the logic engine evaluates this as an immediate command injection pathway:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
interaction_rule(
  (unauthorizedControl(TargetPLC) :-
    execCode(SupervisorHost, admin),
    aclNW(SupervisorHost, TargetPLC, tcp, 502),
    networkService(TargetPLC, _Service, tcp, 502, _Perm)),
  rule_desc('Unauthorized command injection into PLC over unauthenticated Modbus/TCP', 0.9)
).
\end{lstlisting}
This extended rule allows the engine to mathematically prove how a compromised engineering workstation or administrative jump server can bypass traditional security barriers and manipulate physical field equipment directly over unauthenticated operational buses~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.

\paragraph{4. Credential Reuse and Lateral Movement.}
Plant engineers frequently configure identical administrative credentials across jump servers, domain controllers, and auxiliary workstations to simplify site maintenance. The reasoning engine processes firewall permissions alongside custom crosstalk and credential reuse rules to evaluate lateral movement vectors:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
interaction_rule(
  (execCode(TargetHost, admin) :- 
    execCode(CompromisedHost, admin),
    sameCredentials(CompromisedHost, TargetHost)),
  rule_desc('Lateral movement across zones via shared administrative credentials', 1.0)
).
\end{lstlisting}
Through this rule-based approach, the extended logic engine maps how configuration oversights, shared administrative credentials, and unauthorized crosstalk conduits combine to create critical threat vectors, providing a formal framework to assess and harden industrial networks against multi-stage cyberattacks~\cite{Ou05, StanExtended, SunderOT}.

\subsection{Related Application: Firmware Attack Graphs in MIRAGE}
\label{sec:mirage_comparison}
A notable domain application of MulVAL attack graphs beyond conventional enterprise IT networks is established by the MIRAGE (Multi-Binary Image Risk Assessment with Attack Graph Employment) framework introduced by Tayouri et al.~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}.

Traditional vulnerability assessment tools analyze IoT and embedded OT edge devices as monolithic entities~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. However, modern embedded devices run multi-binary embedded Linux distributions (e.g., OpenWrt, BusyBox environments) where operational tasks, such as HTTP web servers, UPnP daemons, DNS forwarders, and proprietary industrial protocol parsers, are partitioned across disparate executable binaries. These distinct binaries execute concurrently under varying privilege boundaries and communicate internally via Inter-Process Communication (IPC) channels, including UNIX domain sockets, FIFO named pipes, shared memory segments, and environment variables.

Tayouri et al. demonstrated that evaluating vulnerabilities within isolated binaries produces severe blind spots~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. An adversary who successfully exploits an initial memory corruption bug in an exposed, network-facing binary (e.g., a lightweight web server) rarely achieves direct root execution immediately. Instead, the attacker initiates an \textit{intranode lateral pivot}: by manipulating local IPC data streams or exploiting shared dynamic libraries (e.g., vulnerable \texttt{libuClibc} routines), the adversary propagates execution across internal binary boundaries, progressively escalating privileges until full device takeover is achieved.

To model intra-device vulnerability chaining, MIRAGE structures risk assessment into a sequential four-stage static analysis pipeline~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Modeler (Firmware Decomposition):} Unpacks compressed firmware filesystem images (SquashFS, CramFS, JFFS2) using Binwalk. It performs static analysis to chart border binaries (daemons bound to external TCP/UDP sockets) and maps internal IPC data dependencies across local binaries.
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability Mapper (NVD Heuristics \& QEMU Emulation):} Identifies software Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs) affecting individual binaries. It queries the NIST National Vulnerability Database (NVD) using static indicator heuristics, version string scraping, and QEMU-emulated command-line execution to emit \texttt{vulExists/5} predicates.
    \item \textbf{Attack Graph Generator (Intranode Logic Engine):} Ingests extracted IPC dependencies and \texttt{vulExists} assertions into an extended MulVAL engine. MIRAGE defines specialized interaction rules that formalize how memory corruption in one binary transfers tainted control flow to adjacent binaries over local IPC channels.
    \item \textbf{Attack Graph Analyzer (EPSS Risk Scoring):} Computes risk scores across derived attack paths by combining binary interaction frequency with Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) probability metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Comparative Analysis: Firmware vs. Plant Footprint.}
While MIRAGE successfully applied MulVAL attack graphs to embedded firmware, its architecture highlights fundamental spatial, semantic, and operational divergences when contrasted with our macroscopic industrial requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Abstraction:} MIRAGE operates strictly at the micro-level within the single-device execution space of a system-on-chip, analyzing Linux kernel IPC tables, UNIX domain sockets, and shared memory segments~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In contrast, our industrial framework operates across a macro-level plant footprint, evaluating multi-tier Purdue Model architectures spanning 21+ virtualized servers, domain controllers, PLCs, and physical drives segmented by inter-zone firewall ACLs (\texttt{aclNW/4}) and Layer-2 fieldbuses (\texttt{l2Connection/5})~\cite{SunderOT}.
    \item \textbf{Ingestion Source:} MIRAGE relies on binary firmware decompilation via Binwalk and static string scraping~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In production industrial manufacturing facilities, security teams do not possess source firmware binaries for proprietary, vendor-locked controllers; instead, they manage high-level configuration management databases and semi-structured JSON asset sheets, necessitating automated neuro-symbolic semantic extraction (LLMASP)~\cite{alviano2025llmasp}.
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability Spectrum:} MIRAGE is built upon a pure software CVE model, querying the NVD to evaluate memory corruption bugs~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}. In operational technology networks, this assumption is fundamentally incomplete, as controllers and drives are routinely compromised without software vulnerabilities. Our framework establishes a hybrid dual-class vulnerability engine, modeling software CVEs alongside exploitless protocol flaws (unauthenticated Modbus/TCP, PROFINET) and systemic credential reuse~\cite{StanExtended, SunderOT}.
    \item \textbf{Analytical Metrics:} While MIRAGE computes probabilistic prioritization via EPSS metrics, statistical exploit prediction models do not exist for unauthenticated industrial protocol flaws (e.g., standard Modbus function codes or Layer-2 PROFINET commands). Consequently, our framework replaces numerical EPSS scoring with formal causal trace extraction and binary counterfactual verification (evaluating whether target goals transition deterministically between $\mathbf{DERIVABLE}$ and $\mathbf{BLOCKED}$ under proposed mitigations).
\end{itemize}

\section{Asset Manifests and Threat Classification}
\label{sec:manifests_threat_class}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before formal reasoning can proceed, the system properties, installed software components, and permissible network boundaries must be captured from plant documentation. Furthermore, candidate attack trajectories must be benchmarked against standardized adversary behavioral taxonomies.

\subsection{SBOMs and Plant-Wide Configuration Manifests}
\label{sec:sboms_vs_manifests}
In modern industrial cybersecurity engineering, tracking deployed software components across heterogeneous devices increasingly involves standardized Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) specifications~\cite{cyclonedx, spdx}. Frameworks such as CycloneDX (OWASP) and SPDX (ISO/IEC 5962) structure package identifiers, component hierarchies, and cryptographic hashes into machine-readable JSON or XML schemas.

While SBOM standards are essential for tracking third-party component supply chains, operational plant auditing requires macroscopic asset and network manifests that capture holistic site architectures. A component-level SBOM lists firmware packages, but typically omits inter-zone firewall access control lists (\texttt{aclNW/4}), shared administrative password hashes (\texttt{sameCredentials/2}), and unauthenticated fieldbus adjacencies (\texttt{l2Connection/5}). Consequently, this research does not ingest micro-level CycloneDX or SPDX feeds directly; instead, it targets the plant-wide engineering configuration artifact (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation, which functions as an architectural surrogate integrating host parameters, network routing matrices, and vulnerability annotations.

\subsection{MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS}
\label{subsec:mitre_ics}

To evaluate industrial cyber-threats beyond abstract reachability graphs, modern security engineering benchmarks attack trajectories against standardized adversary behavioral frameworks~\cite{mitre2024ics}. Developed specifically to capture operational technology threat vectors, the MITRE ATT\&CK for ICS knowledge base (evaluated in its current release) categorizes real-world adversary behaviors across twelve distinct tactics: \textit{Initial Access}, \textit{Execution}, \textit{Persistence}, \textit{Privilege Escalation}, \textit{Defense Evasion}, \textit{Credential Access}, \textit{Discovery}, \textit{Lateral Movement}, \textit{Collection}, \textit{Command and Control}, \textit{Inhibit Response Function}, and \textit{Impair Process Control}.

Unlike enterprise IT attack matrices that heavily emphasize data exfiltration and credential harvesting across business software, ATT\&CK for ICS formalizes domain-specific techniques such as unauthorized command message injection (T0855), controller firmware modification (T0890), and unauthorized parameter manipulation of physical equipment (T0836)~\cite{mitre2024ics}. Correlating formal logic interaction rules directly with ATT\&CK for ICS techniques provides a standardized methodology to translate mathematical attack graph proofs into actionable, defense-in-depth engineering mitigations.

\section{Neuro-Symbolic Approaches to Attack Graph Construction}
\label{sec:neuro_symbolic_frontiers}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bridging the gap between semi-structured configuration manifests and formal logic provers has spurred substantial research combining Large Language Models (LLMs) with symbolic engines~\cite{alviano2025llmasp, WachterSurvey}. However, this literature reflects fundamentally different operational goals depending on whether the framework targets active penetration testing or formal, non-intrusive threat modeling.

\subsection{Active Agentic Red-Teaming}
\label{sec:active_agentic_redteaming}
A prominent direction in recent literature focuses on autonomous, active agents designed to conduct live multi-host network penetration testing. The \textbf{Incalmo} framework~\cite{SingerIncalmo} addresses long-horizon tactical planning in enterprise networks by employing a hierarchical dual-layer architecture: a high-level LLM planner that tracks compromised subnets, paired with a low-level executor generating live Nmap sweeps, Metasploit payloads, and shell commands. Similarly, \textbf{Aurora} (Wang et al.)~\cite{WangSandsToMansions} uses LLMs to extract atomic attack actions from tool documentation and Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) reports, chaining them into causality-preserving multi-step attack scripts using classical Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) solvers. In parallel, frameworks such as \textbf{ATLAS}~\cite{ATLASAgent} focus on auditing agent execution traces, applying semantic abstraction and formal automata learning to extract Markov Chains that explain how autonomous agents make decisions.

While active penetration-testing frameworks demonstrate impressive capabilities in enterprise IT environments, their core operational premise---transmitting active exploit payloads, initiating live shell sessions, and brute-forcing authentication---is strictly incompatible with fragile Operational Technology ecosystems~\cite{SunderOT, TayouriSurvey}. In an industrial facility, an unguided or semi-autonomous agent executing active scans against an embedded PLC risks triggering buffer overruns and fatal controller halts. Consequently, while active red-teaming literature focuses on automated \textit{execution}, this thesis addresses the complementary requirement of \textbf{out-of-band formal modeling}: generating provably complete attack graphs from static manifests without injecting a single packet into live operational networks.

\subsection{Declarative and Formally Validated Attack Modeling}
\label{sec:declarative_formal_modeling}
Complementing active agents, several frameworks investigate declarative logic to formalize attack paths. \textbf{ATAG} (Gandhi et al.)~\cite{GandhiATAG} extends MulVAL's Horn-clause engine to model security risks within Multi-Agent AI Systems (MAAS), formulating custom interaction rules to capture indirect prompt injection and excessive agency cascading across communicating LLM agents. \textbf{EntailLLM} (Mukherji et al.)~\cite{MukherjiEntailLLM} addresses the hallucination problem inherent to generative LLMs by evaluating candidate software vulnerability paths through formal Answer Set Programming (ASP) entailment, discarding ungrounded paths that violate language semantics. Furthermore, Hou et al.~\cite{HouCTI} investigate translating unstructured CTI reports into Datalog interaction rules, extracting reachable attack chains through monotonic reachability analysis.

\subsection{Automated Rule Extraction: The Hydra Baseline}
\label{sec:raise_comparison}
A direct baseline for the LLM-to-MulVAL synthesis problem is established by Stevanovic and Wachter in their research presented at the RAISE workshop, introducing the \textbf{Hydra} framework (also referred to as Hybrid Reasoner) for automating attack graph construction in agentic penetration testing~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}. Their research investigates the scanner-to-symbolic interoperability problem in web security, presenting a semi-automated two-layer pipeline that parses findings from contemporary scanners (Trivy, Semgrep, Nmap) into MulVAL predicates and employs an LLM-assisted process to construct domain-specific Datalog rules grounded in the MITRE ATT\&CK semantic backbone~\cite{mitre_ics}.

While Stevanovic and Wachter establish the baseline feasibility of extracting MulVAL models via LLM assistance, their empirical evaluation across 54 web Capture-the-Flag tasks exposed significant quality and precision limitations. Specifically, their pipeline achieved a mean vulnerability coverage of only \textbf{53.7\%} (with only \textbf{51.9\%} of tasks achieving full coverage) and produced a substantial mean noise-path rate of \textbf{83.9\%}~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}. The authors identified that their architecture operated without automated syntactic or semantic validation, requiring manual inspection of generated Datalog rules and human deduplication of predicates, explicitly defining their approach as semi-automated.

\paragraph{Addressing the Automation Gap.}
This thesis directly addresses this identified research gap. Rather than relying on unconstrained LLM rule generation followed by manual post-processing, this research introduces an automated neuro-symbolic pipeline combining the \textbf{LLMASP} methodology~\cite{alviano2025llmasp} with \textbf{Clingo (ASP)} and \textbf{MulVAL (Datalog)}. By enforcing strict compile-time syntactic and semantic guardrails---including structured YAML schema slicing, automated lowercasing, arity verification, and stable-model completion---this thesis eliminates generated predicate noise, prevents Prolog variable collisions, and establishes a fully automated, formally sound knowledge extraction pipeline tailored to the constraints of Operational Technology.

\subsection{LLMASP: Neural Extraction and Symbolic Reasoning}
\label{sec:llmasp_integration}
While logic-based vulnerability analysis engines provide a scalable framework for evaluating multi-stage attack paths, an automation gap remains in the initial ingestion pipeline~\cite{KonstaSurvey, StevanovicWachterRAISE}. MulVAL requires strictly formatted, relational Datalog ground atoms~\cite{Ou05}. Real-world industrial environments, however, store asset inventories, firewall access control lists (ACLs), and service mappings in semi-structured JSON profiles, CMDB databases, or engineering spreadsheets~\cite{SunderOT}.

To automate this knowledge extraction without relying on brittle, hardcoded regular expression scripts or error-prone unconstrained language models, this research integrates the neuro-symbolic division of labor formalized by Alviano et al. in the LLMASP framework (IJCAI 2025)~\cite{alviano2025llmasp}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System-1 (LLM / Syntactic Extraction):} Large Language Models excel at natural language comprehension, pattern recognition, and syntactic entity extraction. In our architecture, the LLM is restricted strictly to syntactic parsing, converting semi-structured JSON objects into explicit Datalog atom strings based on structured schema bounds.
    \item \textbf{System-2 (Answer Set Programming / Formal Reasoning):} ASP solvers (specifically Clingo~\cite{clingo}) operate over declarative Horn clauses and disjunctive logic programs under stable model semantics. The ASP engine processes background knowledge rules to infer implicit network relationships (such as credential-sharing links and unauthenticated protocol bindings), verify type constraints, and eliminate logical inconsistencies before facts are submitted to MulVAL.
\end{itemize}

By restricting the LLM to syntactic translation and offloading all logical deduction to an ASP/Datalog engine, the framework mitigates generative hallucinations, prevents predicate arity mismatches, and guarantees compile-time logical soundness~\cite{alviano2025llmasp}.

\section{Identified Engineering \& Methodological Problems}
\label{sec:identified_problems}
Building an automated bridge between the plant configuration manifests from Danieli Automation and a formal logic engine reveals five practical challenges. These problems stem from the nature of industrial data storage, token and memory constraints, relational complexity, domain differences in communication protocols, and execution loops in formal solvers.

\subsection{Problem 1: Structural and Syntactic Mismatch in Data Representations}
The first obstacle is the representational gap between how industrial networks are documented and what formal reasoning engines require. 

In modern industrial facilities, asset inventories and network policies are stored in semi-structured, nested formats, such as JSON, CSV tables, or database dumps, to make them easy for human engineers to read and maintain. In the Danieli plant manifest, each machine is an object containing nested arrays for installed software, credential mappings, and firewall conduits, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:json_sample}. 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=json, caption={Sample Raw Industrial Asset Profile from \texttt{configuration.json}}, label={lst:json_sample}]
{
  "id": "jump_server",
  "zone": "ot_dmz",
  "credentials": {
    "admin": "admin_hash_1969"
  },
  "software_services": [
    {
      "name": "sshd",
      "port": 22,
      "protocol": "tcp",
      "privilege": "admin",
      "cve": "cve_2023_38408"
    }
  ],
  "firewall_rules": [
    { "destination": "vdc01", "port": 445, "protocol": "tcp" },
    { "destination": "plc_l1_1", "port": 502, "protocol": "tcp" }
  ]
}
\end{lstlisting}

In contrast, declarative logic tools like MulVAL require a flat relational model composed strictly of typed Datalog ground atoms:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
located(jump_server, ot_dmz, ipSubnet).
hasAccount(jump_server, admin, admin_hash_1969).
networkService(jump_server, sshd, tcp, 22, admin).
vulHost(jump_server, cve_2023_38408, sshd, remoteExploit, privEscalation).
aclNW(jump_server, vdc01, tcp, 445).
aclNW(jump_server, plc_l1_1, tcp, 502).
\end{lstlisting}

Converting one format into the other is difficult to automate reliably:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brittleness of Deterministic Scripts:} Standard procedural scripts relying on hardcoded keys and regular expressions fail whenever documentation formats shift. If an engineer renames a field from \texttt{software\_services} to \texttt{installed\_apps}, changes port values from numbers to strings, or alters the nesting depth of firewall rules, deterministic parsers crash or drop records silently.
    \item \textbf{Syntactic Errors in Generative Models:} Using an unconstrained Large Language Model to write Datalog directly introduces subtle syntax errors. In Prolog and Datalog syntax, identifiers beginning with an uppercase letter are automatically treated as unbound variables rather than constant entities. If a model outputs \texttt{located(Jump\_Server, Ot\_dmz, Subnet)}, the engine interprets the entire statement as an ungrounded variable pattern rather than a concrete host on a subnet, causing logical failures during path derivation. Furthermore, generative models frequently misalign the number of arguments (arity) in standard predicates or hallucinate connections that do not exist in the source file.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problem 2: Context Window Saturation and API Extraction Limits}
The second problem involves the volume of data that must be processed when analyzing a realistic facility. A plant like the Danieli testbed comprises 21 interconnected assets, dozens of services, and a comprehensive cross-zone firewall matrix.

When a complete configuration file is sent to a language model in a single prompt, two issues arise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attentional Degradation across Long Contexts:} While large language models support wide input windows, their accuracy in extracting dense, structured data decreases over long contexts. When presented with a single monolithic JSON document containing hundreds of lines of firewall rules and host objects, models tend to skip isolated attributes, such as a single credential entry, an internal port exception, or a fieldbus identifier.
    \item \textbf{Token Consumption and Rate Limiting:} Repeatedly querying an API with an entire monolithic document to extract different aspects of the plant quickly exhausts token quotas and triggers API rate limits, adding unnecessary latency to the pipeline.
\end{itemize}
The pipeline therefore requires a method to break the input data down into smaller, targeted slices without losing the contextual connections between hosts and their network rules.

\subsection{Problem 3: Combinatorial Overhead in Cross-Host Account Matching}
A frequent lateral movement vector in industrial networks is administrative credential reuse. Plant engineers often use the same local administrative password across jump boxes, domain controllers, and auxiliary workstations to simplify maintenance.

To represent this attack surface formally, the system must declare which hosts share identical credentials using a relation such as \texttt{sameCredentials(HostA, HostB)}. However, determining every instance of credential reuse requires evaluating all pairs of hosts across the network. For an inventory of size $|\mathcal{H}|$, the number of pairwise comparisons grows quadratically:
\begin{equation}
\frac{|\mathcal{H}| \times (|\mathcal{H}| - 1)}{2} = O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)
\end{equation}

Relying on a generative language model to inspect an entire inventory and manually enumerate every matching pair is error-prone. Generative models struggle with exhaustive combinatorial comparisons across large sets, frequently omitting valid matches or inventing pairings that do not share hashes. On the other hand, requiring human plant operators to manually write down every pair in advance defeats the purpose of an automated pipeline. The extraction of basic facts must therefore be decoupled from the combinatorial logic needed to discover shared credentials.

\subsection{Problem 4: Modeling Exploitless Protocols and Industrial Device Realities}
Traditional vulnerability analysis tools were designed around enterprise IT environments. In an IT network, an attacker typically moves from one system to another by exploiting memory corruption bugs (such as buffer overflows in software binaries cataloged as CVEs) to escalate privileges from a basic user to an administrator or root.

In an industrial plant, this abstraction fails to capture how control systems operate and how physical processes are manipulated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Operating System Privileges:} Many lower-tier devices, such as Level~1 Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs) and Level~0 variable frequency drives, run dedicated, embedded firmware without standard multi-user operating systems or login accounts. An adversary does not need to escalate privileges from ``user'' to ``root'' on these controllers.
    \item \textbf{Exploitless Communication Protocols:} Core operational protocols like Modbus/TCP (port 502) and standard PROFINET operate without cryptographic session authentication, packet integrity checks, or encryption by design. Once an adversary obtains network access to the controller's listening port through a firewall exception, they can send valid supervisory commands directly to the hardware. They can alter process setpoints, force emergency stops, or rewrite register values without exploiting any software flaw.
    \item \textbf{Layer-2 Fieldbus Hierarchies:} Actuators and drives on Level~0 are managed directly by PLCs acting as bus masters over Layer-2 industrial networks. Standard IT attack rules do not account for master-slave bus relationships where compromising the controller automatically grants control over downstream physical equipment.
\end{itemize}
Traditional Datalog interaction rules must therefore be extended with domain-specific logic to represent these exploitless protocol realities alongside classical software CVEs.

\subsection{Problem 5: Termination Guarantees and Cyclic Paths in Network Graphs}
The final challenge lies in the execution of the derivation engine itself. Industrial plants frequently include symmetric network access policies, reciprocal connections between supervisory nodes, and mirrored jump hosts for redundancy.

When these relationships are translated into logical rules, such as bidirectional reachability or mutual credential sharing, standard backward-chaining evaluation (like SLD resolution in basic Prolog) can enter infinite loops. If Host $A$ shares credentials with Host $B$, the engine may attempt to prove that Host $A$ can compromise Host $B$, which can compromise Host $A$, leading to non-terminating recursion. 

Without explicit memoization and loop control mechanisms, the derivation engine cannot guarantee termination, preventing the complete synthesis of an attack graph. The reasoning framework must therefore enforce structural tabling to ensure that recursive path exploration terminates reliably in polynomial time.

\section{Dual-Class Vulnerability Categorization}
To address the operational characteristics of the Danieli plant model, security weaknesses within the topology are formalized into two distinct logical categories:

\subsection{Class I: Classical Software CVEs}
Perimeter-facing supervisory daemons and operating system services (e.g., Windows RPC runtime bugs on bastion jump hosts) are represented using a standardized 5-tuple schema:
\begin{equation}
    \text{vulHost}(Host, CVE\_Identifier, ServiceName, ExploitRange, Consequence)
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{HostID}$ identifies the target host entity.
    \item $\text{CVE\_Identifier}$ acts as a descriptive annotation label within the generated graph (e.g., \texttt{cve\_2022\_26809}).
    \item $\text{ExploitRange}$ specifies transport reachability requirements (\texttt{remoteExploit} vs. \texttt{local}).
    \item $\text{Consequence}$ specifies the derived state (\texttt{privEscalation} for code execution, or \texttt{dos} for operational exhaustion).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Class II: Exploitless OT Protocol Flaws \& Architectural Weaknesses}
Within the basic control and physical actuation layers (Levels 1 and 0), compromises are modeled without software memory exploits, capturing three structural weaknesses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unauthenticated Field Protocols:} Modbus/TCP lack of session authentication, permitting direct command injection via reachable ports:
    $$\text{vulLinkProtocol}(\text{HostID}, \text{vul\_modbus\_no\_auth}, \text{modbus}, \text{adjacent}, \text{impersonateDst})$$
    \item \textbf{Systemic Credential Sharing:} Identical administrative hashes shared across maintenance jump servers and domain controllers:
    $$\text{sameCredentials}(\text{Host}_A, \text{Host}_B)$$
    \item \textbf{Unauthenticated Layer-2 Fieldbus Master-Slave Links:} Physical PROFINET actuation paths enabled once master control over a controller is established:
    $$\text{isMaster}(\text{plc\_l1\_1}, \text{bus\_area\_1}) \land \text{isSlave}(\text{drive\_area\_1\_1}, \text{bus\_area\_1})$$
\end{enumerate}

\section{Existing Approaches and Their Limitations in Industrial OT}
\label{sec:existing_solutions_limitations} 

The representational, combinatorial, and operational bottlenecks identified in this chapter are not isolated anomalies of our reference model Danieli manifest; rather, they reflect gaps notices documented across recent attack modeling and cyber-physical security literature. Situating these five challenges within contemporary research clarifies precisely where existing frameworks fall short and motivates the hybrid neuro-symbolic pipeline explored in this thesis.

The structural friction between semi-structured configuration manifests and formal logic representations (Problem~1) is a primary impediment to practical vulnerability verification. In foundational logic frameworks like MulVAL, network topologies and filtering rules are assumed to exist as clean, hand-crafted Horn-clause atoms~\cite{Ou05}. When attempting to automate this translation from modern machine-readable schemas, prior methodologies relied on brittle procedural regex parsers that break under minor field shifts, or unconstrained LLMs that introduce syntactic hallucinations. 

The recent contribution by Stevanovic and Wachter~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE} highlights the practical challenges of integrating language models into symbolic threat modeling. Studying LLM-assisted rule generation for offensive security, the lack of compile-time syntactic guardrails was identified as a key limitation of existing pipelines. According to the authors, their semi-automated rule-generation workflow encountered variable-casing anomalies and duplicate variable assignments that had to be resolved in postprocessing steps and with human-in-the-loop rule inspection. Furthermore, the high noise rate reported in their evaluation measured false positives against empirical ground-truth vulnerabilities present in the target environment (primarily driven by noisy scanners), rather than Prolog syntax failures. In their architecture, fact initialization remained deterministic, necessitating manual tool-output parsing, fact correlation, and normalization. Our research builds directly on these insights: rather than evaluating unconstrained rule synthesis, this thesis automates the preceding fact-ingestion and normalization layer via a neuro-symbolic pipeline, enforcing compile-time guardrails to prevent variable-casing collisions and arity errors before facts reach the symbolic reasoner.

This issue is directly exacerbated by context window degradation and token consumption limits (Problem~2). While long-context LLMs have expanded input capacities, empirical evaluations by Hou et al.~\cite{HouCTI} and Mukherji et al.~\cite{MukherjiEntailLLM} demonstrate that attentional fidelity degrades significantly when dense configuration records or extensive threat intelligence texts are processed monolithically. Critical isolated attributes, such as single firewall exceptions or specific credential tokens, are frequently dropped. Existing automated pipelines offer no principled mechanism to prune industrial configuration topologies prior to prompt submission, confirming the necessity of the targeted payload slicing strategy formulated in our approach.

Similarly, the combinatorial bottleneck of pairwise credential matching (Problem~3) represents an unresolved friction point in automated lateral movement modeling. In standard enterprise threat models, identity relationships are either manually annotated or flattened into basic reachability matrices~\cite{Ou05, WachterSurvey}. When automated red-teaming agents (such as Incalmo~\cite{SingerIncalmo}) attempt to evaluate credential reuse across multi-host environments, they rely on heuristic exploration that scales combinatorially ($O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)$), often skipping subtle cross-tier access conduits. Literature in declarative verification lacks an automated bridge capable of separating basic identity extraction from the exhaustive, quadratic equivalence closure required to formalize shared credentials, a gap this work explicitly bridges using Answer Set Programming under stable model semantics.

At the physical and protocol layers, the failure of traditional frameworks to account for exploitless communication flaws and embedded device realities (Problem~4) is widely recognized. While landmark extensions such as the MIRAGE framework by Tayouri et al.~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE} demonstrated that MulVAL can model embedded devices, MIRAGE focused strictly on micro-architectural, multi-binary firmware vulnerabilities via QEMU emulation and software CVE lookup. In operational plant environments, however, Stan et al.~\cite{StanExtended} and Sunder et al.~\cite{SunderOT} emphasize that adversarial compromise rarely hinges on classical software exploits once reachability is attained. Core fieldbus protocols like Modbus/TCP and Layer-2 PROFINET operate without cryptographic authentication or integrity verification by design. Despite these insights, existing automated synthesis pipelines remain fundamentally IT- and CVE-centric, lacking native predicates to link supervisory code execution directly to physical fieldbus actuation without an intermediate software vulnerability.

Finally, non-terminating cyclic recursion during backward-chaining (Problem~5) continues to constrain formal path generation over symmetric topologies. As surveyed by Konsta et al.~\cite{KonstaSurvey} and Wachter~\cite{WachterSurvey}, reciprocal reachability rules between redundant jump hosts or mirrored supervisory nodes frequently cause standard SLD resolution in Prolog engines to fall into infinite loops. While tabled resolution algorithms (SLG resolution in XSB) theoretically resolve cyclic dependencies~\cite{swift2012xsb}, real-world implementations routinely fail to configure memoization directives across custom-authored industrial interaction predicates, resulting in derivation timeouts on realistic plant topologies.

In summary, while individual facets of industrial threat modeling have been investigated in isolation, such as firmware decompilation in MIRAGE~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}, semi-automated rule extraction in RAISE~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}, and conceptual protocol rules in Stan et al.~\cite{StanExtended}, no existing work provides an end-to-end, out-of-band pipeline that simultaneously resolves syntactic ingestion noise, combinatorial identity matching, and exploitless Purdue-level attack graph derivation. This thesis specifically closes these interconnected literature gaps.

\chapter{System Architecture \& Methodology}
\label{chap:architecture}

To address the representational and operational challenges outlined in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, this thesis explores an automated, out-of-band threat modeling pipeline. Rather than relying on live network probing or unconstrained language models, the approach combines prompt-constrained syntactic extraction, declarative Answer Set Programming, and tabled Horn-clause logic derivation. This chapter defines the underlying threat model, establishes the mathematical system formalisms, and walks through each phase of the methodology, discussing the practical rationales behind each design decision.

\section{Formal Attacker and Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat_model}
To establish mathematically verifiable security bounds, we define an explicit threat model governing the adversary's capabilities, initial access, and lateral progression boundaries across the industrial plant topology. 

The rationale for framing the threat model around external perimeter ingress and subsequent internal progression was established through consultations with plant and automation engineers at Danieli Automation. In contemporary steel manufacturing facilities, an external adversary pivoting from untrusted enterprise boundaries through the DMZ into the basic control layer represents the primary operational risk. Furthermore, to identify the most critical lateral movement mechanisms, we reviewed common ICS incident patterns and the Purdue Reference Model via domain literature and expert discussions. This analysis highlighted that adversarial propagation within internal OT zones rarely hinges on zero-day exploits, but rather exploits operational realities such as shared administrative credentials and unauthenticated field protocols (e.g., Modbus/TCP, PROFINET). Consequently, the attacker profile and interaction rules were engineered through a domain-expert-guided process to mirror these specific operational risks.
\subsection{Adversary Profile and Capabilities}
We model an external, computationally bounded adversary originating outside the plant's operational perimeter. The adversary's initial execution capabilities are restricted strictly to the untrusted network perimeter, formally asserted as $\text{attackerLocated}(\text{internet}) \land \text{malicious}(\text{internet})$. 

In terms of initial intelligence, the adversary possesses no prior cryptographic credentials, authentication tokens, or internal topological maps, but is assumed capable of conducting reconnaissance against externally reachable endpoints, such as administrative ports exposed through the Industrial Demilitarized Zone (IDMZ). The adversary is equipped with functional exploits for publicly cataloged software vulnerabilities (Class~I CVEs) affecting these exposed services. 

Once an intermediate host $H \in \mathcal{H}$ is compromised, achieving administrative execution denoted as $\text{execCode}(H, \text{admin})$, the adversary inherits the reachability permissions granted to that host by the plant firewall access control matrix. Furthermore, gaining control of a host provides access to its local credential storage, enabling lateral credential reuse across any systems that share identical password hashes, represented by $\text{sameCredentials}(H, H')$ relations. 

Finally, upon acquiring either administrative execution or unauthenticated network reachability to an industrial controller, the adversary can leverage native field protocol characteristics (Class~II flaws). This allows the adversary to transmit unauthenticated command frames across Modbus/TCP or Layer-2 PROFINET links to alter setpoints or manipulate physical field equipment without needing software memory exploits.

\subsection{Adversary Objective Function}
The objective of the adversary is to transition the industrial plant from a nominal operating state into one or more unauthorized operational states within a target set $\mathcal{G}_{target} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_D$. Formally, the threat model evaluates whether the logical derivation engine can satisfy:
\begin{equation}
\centering
    \exists \tau = \langle r_1, r_2, \dots, r_k \rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{F}_{complete} \vdash_{\tau} g, \quad g \in \mathcal{G}_{target}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ represents a finite sequence of interaction rule applications from the domain ruleset $\mathcal{R}_{OT}$. These derivations evaluate whether the adversary can attain terminal goals such as Active Directory compromise ($\text{domainCompromise}(\text{danieli\_domain})$), unauthorized command injection into plant controllers ($\text{unauthorizedControl}(\text{plc\_l1\_1})$), or direct manipulation of variable frequency drives \\($\text{physicalDriveControl}(\text{jump\_server}, \text{drive\_area\_1\_1})$).

\subsection{Trust Assumptions and Security Invariants}
The formal verification model relies on three structural invariants:
First, we enforce a \textit{Firewall Enforcement Invariant}, assuming that network filtering hardware deterministically enforces the access control matrix $\mathcal{A}_{CL}$ declared in configuration manifests, such that network traffic cannot cross zone boundaries unless explicitly permitted by an $\text{aclNW}(Src, Dst, Prot, Port)$ clause. 
Second, we reflect \textit{Embedded Device Fragility} by recognizing that Level~1 controllers and Level~0 fieldbus drives operate without protocol-layer cryptographic authentication; thus, any host capable of routing packets to an industrial port is trusted by the controller to issue operational commands. 
Third, following standard logic programming conventions, the framework assumes \textit{Monotonic Privilege Accumulation}, meaning that privileges, access tokens, and reachability acquired during an intrusion sequence remain valid throughout the multi-stage evaluation.

\section{Mathematical System Model \& Multi-Layer Graph Formalism}
\label{sec:system_model}
We represent the industrial plant infrastructure as a multi-tier cyber-physical system model, structured around formal relational sets.

\subsection{Industrial Plant Model Formalization}
The industrial automation plant is formalized as a 5-tuple:
\begin{equation}
    \centering
    \mathcal{S} = (\mathcal{H}, \mathcal{Z}, \mathcal{L}, \mathcal{F}_{bus}, \mathcal{A}_{CL})
\end{equation}
The set $\mathcal{H} = \{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_n\}$ represents the finite collection of physical and virtual host entities, including supervisory servers, domain controllers, operator workstations, industrial controllers, and field drives. 

The spatial partitioning of the plant is defined by \\$\mathcal{Z} = \{\text{internet}, \text{ot\_dmz}, \text{danieli\_domain}, \text{level\_2\_supervisory}, \text{level\_1\_network}, \text{fieldbus\_l0}\}$, which maps directly to the functional security zones of the Purdue Reference Model via a surjective mapping function $\zeta: \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{Z}$. 

Active transport services are represented by the relation $\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{S}_{prog} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot} \times \mathbb{N} \times \mathcal{P}_{riv}$, where each element $(h, s, p, port, priv)$ specifies an active listening daemon $s$ bound to protocol $p \in \{\text{tcp}, \text{udp}\}$ on transport port $port$, executing under privilege level $priv \in \{\text{user}, \text{admin}, \text{root}\}$. 

Physical industrial communications at lower layers are captured by $\mathcal{F}_{bus} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{B}_{id} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot}$, defining Layer-2 fieldbus connections between master controllers and slave actuators. 

Finally, the network access control policy is defined by the matrix $\mathcal{A}_{CL} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{H} \times \mathcal{P}_{rot} \times \mathbb{N}$, which formalizes the permissible communication channels established across inter-zone firewalls.

\subsection{Bipartite Logical Attack Graph Semantics}
The threat modeling pipeline compiles these environmental relations into a directed bipartite Logical Attack Graph (LAG):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}_{AND} \cup \mathcal{V}_{OR}, \mathcal{E})
\end{equation}
The vertices of the graph are partitioned into two distinct semantic classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Derivation Nodes ($\mathcal{V}_{AND}$):} These represent instantiated interaction rules from the extended domain logic $\mathcal{R}_{OT}$. An AND-node is logically satisfied if and only if all of its incoming precondition child nodes evaluate to true:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{val}(v_{AND}) = \bigwedge_{(u, v_{AND}) \in \mathcal{E}} \text{val}(u)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{State Nodes ($\mathcal{V}_{OR}$):} These denote either primitive configuration facts ($v \in \mathcal{V}_P$) or derived privilege states ($v \in \mathcal{V}_D$). An OR-node is satisfied if at least one incoming derivation rule is satisfied:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{val}(v_{OR}) = \bigvee_{(v_{AND}, v_{OR}) \in \mathcal{E}} \text{val}(v_{AND})
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Instead of employing an unconstrained end-to-end language model or rigid procedural regular expressions, the methodology partitions the threat modeling task into five focused steps.

\subsection{Step 1: Targeted Payload Slicing and Syntactic Ingestion}
\label{subsec:step1_ingestion}
The first phase of the pipeline ingests the semi-structured industrial configuration manifest (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation. Submitting an entire industrial manifest into a single large language model prompt often leads to token limits and attentional degradation across long contexts. Conversely, deterministic scripts break when field names or object hierarchies change across plant management tools.

To address this challenge, our architecture adapts the neuro-symbolic division of labor from the LLMASP framework. Here, the language model is restricted strictly to an inductive syntactic parser (System-1). Rather than passing the full JSON file on every query, the pipeline controller (\texttt{run\_llmasp.py}) applies \textit{targeted payload slicing}. 

The controller reads the extraction targets declared in \texttt{application\_ot.yaml} and extracts only the specific subtrees required for each target relation. For instance, when parsing network reachability rules (\texttt{aclNW/4}), the prompt receives only the \texttt{firewall\_rules} array. When extracting physical Layer-2 fieldbuses (\texttt{l2Connection/5}), only the \texttt{fieldbus\_connections} block is submitted. Similarly, when querying host-level attributes, such as zone placement, listening services, and local credential hashes, the model receives only the pruned \texttt{hosts} array. This slicing strategy reduces per-request prompt volume by up to 65\%, keeping token counts well within API limits and preventing the model from missing isolated configuration attributes.

\subsection{Step 2: Semantic Normalization and Syntactic Guardrails}
Because generative models can introduce subtle formatting inconsistencies, the pipeline intercepts all output emitted by System-1 prior to any logical reasoning. In Datalog and Prolog semantics, any identifier starting with a capital letter is treated as an unbound variable rather than an atomic constant. If an extraction query produces an atom like \texttt{located(Jump\_Server, Ot\_dmz, IpSubnet)}, the reasoning engine fails to ground the host to a specific zone, invalidating subsequent derivations.

The pipeline controller enforces automated \textit{compile-time guardrails} to prevent these syntax issues. All extracted strings, host names, and protocol identifiers are automatically converted into lowercase alphanumeric characters separated by underscores. 

In addition, the controller validates the arity and type signatures of each extracted atom against expected schemas, verifying that predicates like \texttt{networkService} contain exactly five arguments and that transport ports are valid numerical values. Any malformed, partial, or out-of-schema outputs are discarded immediately, producing a clean, verified base of explicit facts ($\mathcal{F}_{explicit}$).

\subsection{Step 3: Declarative Knowledge Completion via Clingo}
After explicit facts are parsed, the system must derive the implicit architectural relationships across the plant. As established in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, calculating administrative password sharing across an entire network scales quadratically ($O(|\mathcal{H}|^2)$) with the number of hosts. Prompting a generative language model to enumerate every valid pair across 21 interconnected machines introduces severe hallucination risks and omitted links.

Rather than relying on neural inference for relational completion, this step is offloaded to an Answer Set Programming solver, specifically Clingo~5.x (System-2A). Operating under the Closed World Assumption with stable model semantics, Clingo processes declarative background knowledge rules ($\mathcal{K}_{KB}$):
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Prolog]
sameCredentials(H1, H2) :- 
    hasAccount(H1, U, P), 
    hasAccount(H2, U, P), 
    H1 != H2.

isDomainController(H, danieli_domain) :- 
    networkService(H, active_directory, _, _, _).

vulLinkProtocol(H, vul_modbus_no_auth, modbus, adjacent, impersonateDst) :- 
    networkService(H, modbus_service, _, _, _).

isMaster(H, bus_area_1) :- 
    networkService(H, modbus_service, tcp, 502, _).

isSlave(D, bus_area_1) :- 
    located(D, fieldbus_l0, _).
\end{lstlisting}

By executing this logic declaratively, Clingo evaluates all pairwise credential matches, classifies Domain Controllers, and formalizes unauthenticated protocol bindings in approximately $0.0060$ seconds with formal soundness. This step eliminates manual pairing overhead while preventing generative hallucinations. The resulting stable model is then combined with static axioms to materialize the closed Datalog knowledge base, saved as \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}.

\subsection{Step 4: Monotonic Derivation with MulVAL and Tabled XSB}
In the third phase, the materialized fact base is ingested by the MulVAL reasoning engine (System-2B), executed on an underlying XSB Prolog environment. 

MulVAL evaluates the completed environment against our extended domain ruleset, \texttt{attack1.P}. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, real-world industrial configurations often feature reciprocal firewall access, bidirectional communication conduits, or mutual credential sharing. Under standard Prolog SLD resolution, these symmetric relationships cause infinite backward-chaining loops. 

To guarantee termination, the execution environment enforces \textit{XSB Tabling Directives} on intermediate derived predicates, such as \texttt{:- table execCode/2.} and \texttt{:- table netAccess/5.}. Tabling provides memoization: once a specific subgoal is derived for a host or zone, its truth value is cached internally. When the backward-chaining engine encounters that subgoal again through an alternate or cyclical path, it retrieves the cached result directly rather than re-evaluating the rule tree. This ensures that cyclic reachability paths terminate reliably and that attack graphs compile in low polynomial time ($O(N^2)$).

\subsection{Step 5: Graph Materialization and Counterfactual Hardening}
The final phase of the pipeline formats the logical derivation trees into practical security artifacts. The engine outputs graphical attack trees in PDF and Graphviz DOT formats for visual inspection, generates detailed textual execution traces mapping the step-by-step path from perimeter to target, and logs execution timing benchmarks.

Beyond diagnosing vulnerabilities, the pipeline functions as a tool for \textit{counterfactual verification}. By adjusting specific properties in the initial JSON configuration, such as revoking a direct maintenance firewall conduit or replacing shared administrative passwords with unique credentials, plant engineers can re-run the pipeline to observe whether the corresponding attack paths collapse. This allows security teams to verify the effectiveness of proposed mitigations mathematically before executing any configuration changes on live machinery.

\section{Architectural Lineage and Design Rationales}
\label{sec:design_rationales}

\subsection{Methodological Lineage: Adapting the Five-Step Shape}
The organizational workflow of our pipeline follows the five-step progression established in recent attack graph literature, particularly the MIRAGE framework: asset modeling, vulnerability mapping, interaction derivation, and path analysis~\cite{TayouriMIRAGE}.

However, as analyzed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, MIRAGE was engineered for micro-level firmware auditing, decomposing individual device binary images using Binwalk and QEMU-emulated CVE version lookups. In contrast, our pipeline adapts this four-stage structure to macroscopic industrial plant topologies with three deliberate architectural departures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We replace binary firmware decompilation with prompt-constrained semantic extraction over semi-structured configuration manifests (LLMASP).
    \item We replace pure software CVE databases with a dual-class vulnerability model that incorporates unauthenticated field protocols and systemic credential reuse.
    \item We replace probabilistic EPSS risk scoring with deterministic counterfactual satisfiability. Because statistical exploit prediction scores do not exist for unauthenticated protocol interactions (such as native Modbus/TCP or Layer-2 PROFINET commands), our fourth stage focuses on formal logical verification, proving mathematically whether designated mitigations break the derivation trees across the Purdue hierarchy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why LLMASP over End-to-End Fine-Tuned LLMs?}
A possible alternative architecture would involve fine-tuning an end-to-end language model to ingest the configuration file and directly output attack graphs in Graphviz DOT format. We explicitly chose not to use an end-to-end neural approach based on three practical considerations:

First, generative language models operate probabilistically, predicting output tokens based on statistical likelihood rather than formal mathematical deduction. When tasked with multi-hop reachability reasoning across complex networks, they frequently hallucinate plausible-looking attack paths that violate underlying firewall rules. In industrial automation, where assessments inform operational safety, threat models must be formally sound and verifiable.

Second, configuration schemas vary across automation vendors, such as differences between Siemens TIA Portal outputs, Rockwell manifests, and Danieli Automation inventories. An end-to-end fine-tuned model overfits to specific naming conventions and requires retraining when fields change. In contrast, prompt-guided semantic extraction (System-1) adapts dynamically to different schema layouts through lightweight prompt adjustments without model retraining.

Third, safety-critical industrial operations require explainability. End-to-end neural generation functions as a black box, making it difficult to audit why a particular path was generated. In our neuro-symbolic framework, all causal reasoning is delegated to a deterministic logic engine, ensuring that every transition in the derived attack graph is supported by a formal Horn-clause derivation trace.

\subsection{Hybrid Solver Rationale: ASP (Clingo) vs. Tabled Datalog (MulVAL)}
A key architectural choice in our pipeline is coupling Answer Set Programming with Horn-clause Datalog, leveraging Clingo and MulVAL for their respective strengths:

Clingo operates under the Closed World Assumption with stable model semantics, making it well-suited for non-monotonic reasoning, constraint satisfaction, and declarative entity completion. We utilize Clingo in System-2A to compute quadratic credential-sharing relations ($\text{sameCredentials}/2$), identify domain controller roles, and formalize unauthenticated protocol bindings under closed-world assumptions.

In contrast, MulVAL executes Horn-clause Datalog under monotonic logic, using goal-directed SLG resolution with tabling in XSB Prolog. This reasoning engine is optimized for polynomial-time transitive reachability queries across multi-hop firewall boundaries, constructing causal dependency graphs without being trapped by cyclic dependencies. Partitioning these tasks allows each declarative engine to handle the computational phase for which it is best suited.

\subsection{Theoretical Soundness \& Semantic Guardrails}
To ensure that stochastic inconsistencies from the language model cannot propagate into the symbolic reasoning engines, the pipeline enforces strict syntactic and semantic checks. 

The ingestion controller intercepts all raw text, normalizing strings to lowercase alphanumeric identifiers to avoid collisions with Prolog variable definitions. All generated atoms are validated against arity and type signatures, ensuring that malformed facts are discarded prior to ASP grounding. 

Finally, Clingo's grounding engine processes these atoms under stable model semantics, isolating any contradictory or ungrounded assertions. This ensures that only logically coherent, strictly typed facts are admitted into \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}, preserving the formal integrity of the downstream derivation engine.

\section{System-2: Declarative Symbolic Completion and Deductive Derivation}
\label{sec:system2_implementation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

System-2 is partitioned into two distinct declarative engines: Answer Set Programming in Clingo for background knowledge materialization (System-2A), and Datalog derivation in MulVAL for attack graph synthesis (System-2B)~\cite{alviano2025llmasp}.

\subsection{System-2A: Declarative ASP Materialization via \textsc{Clingo}}
A key design choice of our methodology is offloading relational deduction and entity linking to Answer Set Programming~\cite{clingo}. 

As identified in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}, checking administrative credential sharing across a network requires pairwise comparisons over all host accounts, scaling quadratically as $O(\vert{}\mathcal{H}\vert{}^2)$. Asking a language model to find every matching pair across 21 machines often results in missed connections and hallucinated links. In our pipeline, the model extracts only the basic account facts (\texttt{hasAccount/3}). Deducing shared credentials is then handled symbolically by Clingo~5.x using background knowledge rules $\mathcal{K}_{KB}$ under stable model semantics:

\begin{align}
    \text{sameCredentials}(H_1, H_2) &\leftarrow \text{hasAccount}(H_1, U, P) \land \text{hasAccount}(H_2, U, P) \land H_1 \neq H_2 \\
    \text{isDomainController}(H, \text{danieli\_domain}) &\leftarrow \text{networkService}(H, \text{active\_directory}, \_, \_, \_) \\
    \text{vulLinkProtocol}(H, \text{vul\_modbus\_no\_auth}, &\text{modbus}, \text{adjacent}, \text{impersonateDst}) \leftarrow \nonumber \\
    &\quad \text{networkService}(H, \text{modbus\_service}, \text{tcp}, 502, \_) \\
    \text{isMaster}(H, \text{bus\_area\_1}) &\leftarrow \text{networkService}(H, \text{modbus\_service}, \text{tcp}, 502, \_) \\
    \text{isSlave}(D, \text{bus\_area\_1}) &\leftarrow \text{located}(D, \text{fieldbus\_l0}, \_)
\end{align}

Regarding the provenance of these extended Purdue rules, they were constructed using a \textbf{hybrid human-in-the-loop methodology}: the architectural structures and protocol behaviors were conceptually derived from foundational industrial cybersecurity guidelines (such as the Purdue Model and Danieli plant specifications), and subsequently formalized and validated manually as Datalog rules.

This declarative step serves three specific functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Equation (5.3) calculates the symmetric transitive closure of credential sharing across all hosts. It identifies when different machines share administrative password hashes without requiring the language model to perform pairwise comparisons.
    \item Equation (5.4) identifies which hosts run Active Directory services and marks them as Domain Controllers, formalizing their role for domain-takeover rules.
    \item Equations (5.5) through (5.7) inspect active services and subnet locations to bind unauthenticated Modbus protocols to controllers and establish master-slave relationships over Layer-2 fieldbuses.
\end{itemize}
Clingo evaluates these rules in approximately $0.0060$ seconds, ensuring that all relational links are derived quickly and without hallucinations~\cite{clingo}. The resulting stable model is then combined with static axioms to produce the closed fact base: \texttt{environmentNetwork.P}.

\subsection{Vulnerability Modeling Mechanics: Static 5-Tuples vs. Dynamic NVD Ingestion}
In our framework, vulnerability data enters the pipeline as pre-classified 5-tuples:
\begin{equation}
    \text{vulHost}(Host, CVE\_Identifier, ServiceName, ExploitRange, Consequence)
\end{equation}

Within the symbolic Datalog engine, the $CVE\_Identifier$ parameter (e.g., \texttt{cve\_2022\_26809}) is semantically inert, serving strictly as an explanatory annotation label within attack graph nodes~\cite{Ou05}. Conceptually, it is vital to distinguish between a \textit{vulnerability} and an \textit{exploit}: a vulnerability represents a static system condition (a weakness in software or protocol design), whereas an exploit constitutes an active operational action required for the vulnerability to manifest. 

Deductive inference relies entirely on exact term unification of $ExploitRange$ (\texttt{remoteExploit}) and $Consequence$ (\texttt{privEscalation} vs. \texttt{dos}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item When $Consequence = \text{privEscalation}$, the fact is consumed by Rule~2 to derive arbitrary code execution (\texttt{execCode/2}).
    \item When $Consequence = \text{dos}$, the fact unifies exclusively with Rule~7, deriving control-loop denial of service (\texttt{dos/2}).
\end{itemize}

This decoupling of the vulnerability string from the logical consequence ensures that attack graph generation remains mathematically sound and independent of external API connectivity or database schema changes during evaluation~\cite{Ou05}.

The assigned weight of $1.0$ reflects deterministic service termination upon successful transmission of the exploit payload.

    \item \textbf{Fieldbus and Actuator Control Transitions (Rule~8):} Manufacturing shop floors rely on deterministic Layer-2 fieldbuses (e.g., PROFINET) that operate below the IP routing layer. Rule~8 bridges logical network compromise with physical mechanical actuation: an adversary possessing execution on an adjacent node can actuate an industrial drive (\texttt{physicalDriveControl}) if they have gained unauthorized control over the designated fieldbus Master PLC and share an unrouted Layer-2 bus topology with the slave device. This rule formalizes the causal chain through which logical compromise in the supervisory zone propagates down into physical actuators.

    \item \textbf{Industrial Telemetry Tampering (Rule~9):} Operational situational awareness depends on historical process monitoring systems (such as ibaPDA and ibaHD-Server). Rule~9 models data integrity attacks across bidirectional measurement streams: if an attacker compromises a data acquisition host, they can intercept and forge recorded physical parameters before ingestion by the central historian, deceiving supervisory operators into erroneous manual interventions. The probability weight ($0.8$) captures the operational requirement that the adversary must craft syntactically valid measurement frames without causing channel dropouts that would trigger loss-of-signal alarms.
\end{itemize}

By evaluating these rules over the materialized fact base using tabled backward-chaining, MulVAL derives complete, explainable attack graphs without entering cyclic non-terminating loops~\cite{Ou05, swift2012xsb}. Note that while these derived attack paths are \textit{complete with respect to the given knowledge base} (meaning the engine reaches a logical fixed point where no new facts can be derived), the framework does \textit{not} claim completeness with respect to real-world ground-truth vulnerability coverage; if a rule or configuration fact is omitted from the knowledge base, the corresponding attack path cannot be derived.

\section{Evaluation Methodology, Metrics, and Epistemological Ground Truth}
\label{sec:methodology_ground_truth}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To evaluate the pipeline rigorously, we must clarify the metrics used to measure performance and address how security verification is validated in industrial operational environments where classical ground truth datasets do not exist.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics Definition}
The performance, scalability, and quality of the pipeline are evaluated across five primary dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Syntactic Parsing Precision and Ingestion Latency ($T\_{\text{neural}}$):} Measures the wall-clock execution time required by System-1 to parse targeted JSON subtrees into typed ground atoms, alongside the syntactic adherence of emitted atoms to the schema signatures defined in Table~\ref{tab:predicate_signatures}.
    \item \textbf{Declarative Completion Latency ($T\_{\text{asp}}$):} Measures the computational duration required by Clingo 5.x to ground and solve the non-monotonic background rules ($\mathcal{K}_{KB}$), materializing implicit pairwise credential matches and protocol vulnerability bindings under stable model semantics.
    \item \textbf{Goal-Directed Derivation Time ($T\_{\text{mulval}}$):} Measures the execution duration required by MulVAL running on XSB Prolog to resolve backward-chaining queries, construct the proof tree, and compile the Logical Attack Graph (LAG).
    \item \textbf{Derivation Complexity and Edge Density ($\vert{}\mathcal{E}\vert{}$):} Quantifies the structural size and density of the synthesized attack graph, tracking the number of logical dependency edges connecting AND derivation steps to OR state nodes.
    \item \textbf{Counterfactual Collapse Rate:} Measures whether the derivation trees for target attack goals collapse from a satisfiable status (\textbf{DERIVABLE}) to an unsatisfiable status (\textbf{BLOCKED}) when specific firewall conduits or credential links are computationally revoked.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Ground Truth Dilemma in Operational Technology Network Hardening}
In conventional empirical computer science, such as natural language processing or image classification, systems are evaluated against an established ground truth test set using statistical metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-score~\cite{WachterSurvey}. Similarly, in recent agentic web-security benchmarks such as Cybench~\cite{cybench2024} and the evaluation conducted by Stevanovic and Wachter~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}, ground truth is established by deploying Capture-The-Flag (CTF) challenges where the exact exploitable vulnerability sequence is documented in advance by the challenge authors. In related domains such as incident response, recent work has leveraged MulVAL graphs to derive and select response playbooks across fixed test scenarios with synthetic fact bases~\cite{gurabi2026playbooks}. 

However, our research focuses specifically on \textbf{network hardening in operational technology} (pre-attack proactive prevention), rather than post-hoc incident response or active red-teaming. In live industrial automation, an active steel manufacturing plant operating continuous casting machinery or rolling mills cannot be subjected to live cyberattacks, penetration testing exploits, or intentional denial-of-service disruptions to establish an empirical baseline of compromised states~\cite{SunderOT, TayouriSurvey}. 

Consequently, in pre-attack OT network hardening, ground truth cannot be an empirical trace of physical destruction. Instead, evaluation is conducted relative to a \textbf{Structural Reference Artifact} and governed by \textbf{Formal Fixpoint Semantics}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fidelity Relative to the Reference Artifact:} The operational ground reference is defined by the production engineering configuration manifest (\texttt{configuration.json}) provided by Danieli Automation. A pipeline extraction is accurate relative to this reference artifact if and only if every emitted ground fact corresponds to an actual declared configuration property, without omitting declared firewall rules or inventing non-existent daemons.
    \item \textbf{Fixpoint Computation and Stable Model Semantics:} Rather than measuring statistical probability, the validity of derived attack paths is established through fixpoint computation over finite Herbrand models. In Horn-clause Datalog, an attack goal $g$ is derived if and only if $g$ belongs to the least fixpoint of the immediate consequence operator $T_{\mathcal{R}}$ applied to the complete ground fact base:
    \begin{equation}
        g \in \text{lfp}(T_{\mathcal{R}})(\mathcal{F}_{\text{complete}}) = \bigcup_{k \ge 0} T_{\mathcal{R}}^k(\mathcal{F}_{\text{complete}})
    \end{equation}
    where $T_{\mathcal{R}}(I) = I \ cup \{ H \mid (H \leftarrow B_1, \dots, B_m) \in \text{ground}(\mathcal{R}_{OT}) \land \{B_1, \dots, B_m\} \subseteq I \}$.
    Mathematically, this represents a fixpoint under forward-chaining application of the immediate consequence operator, which is computed efficiently in goal-directed backward-chaining via SLG resolution with tabling in XSB Prolog~\cite{swift2012xsb}. Simultaneously, System-2A computes stable models in Answer Set Programming (ASP)~\cite{clingo}. Because Systems 2A and 2B operate strictly through exact symbolic fixpoint and stable model search, every edge in the synthesized attack graph represents a verified logical entailment. The concept of an ungrounded or hallucinated attack path is mathematically impossible within this symbolic layer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis presented an end-to-end, neuro-symbolic framework for automated cyber-physical risk assessment in industrial Operational Technology (OT) environments, evaluated against the reference architecture of a Danieli automation plant. Industrial control networks present unique verification challenges due to their hybrid architectures, governed by the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture, where IT enterprise infrastructure directly converges with deterministic fieldbus protocols, programmable logic controllers (PLCs), and physical actuators. 
Traditional threat modeling methodologies in OT rely heavily on manual approaches and static vulnerability scanning, both of which struggle to capture the complex, multi-stage causality spanning logical perimeter breaches down to Layer~0 physical actuation.

To address these limitations, our approach decoupled network threat modeling into a structured, three-tier neuro-symbolic pipeline. In the first stage, the neural extraction phase (System-1 via LLMASP) decomposed the ingestion task across seven discrete, prompt-sliced predicate definitions. An open Large Language Model extracted explicit topological primitives, firewall rules, credentials, software CVEs, and data flows directly from unstructured configuration inputs in $51.16$ seconds without structural hallucination. Subsequently, the symbolic completion stage (System-2A via \textsc{Clingo}) grounded background domain constraints in $0.0060$ seconds, computing transitive equivalence closures over shared credentials and fieldbus topologies to yield 90 ground Datalog facts without generative drift. In the final stage, formal attack graph synthesis (System-2B via MulVAL and XSB Prolog) executed domain-adapted industrial interaction rules using SLG resolution to synthesize sound, causal attack graphs.

The empirical evaluation across eight distinct operational attack scenarios, spanning initial DMZ ingress, transitive domain controller compromise, unauthenticated Modbus/TCP command injection, OPC UA denial-of-service, and telemetry falsification on the Level-2 historian, achieved 100\% derivation coverage. Derivation latencies remained under $1.4$ seconds per scenario, confirming that formal, causal safety guarantees can be derived deterministically from automated network extractions. The resulting pipeline was fully containerized and instrumented with deterministic replay mechanisms to guarantee academic reproducibility.

\section{Future Works}
\label{sec:future_works}

While the current framework demonstrates the validity and formal soundness of neuro-symbolic attack graph generation, several avenues remain for extension.

A primary direction concerns fine-grained lateral transport modeling. The current credential-reuse interaction rules assume administrative reachability whenever identical credential hashes are discovered across hosts. Future work should couple credential propagation rules with strict network segmentation predicates, such as verifying explicit transport conduits for RDP, WinRM, SSH, or SMB across Purdue levels, to capture micro-segmentation policies more faithfully. Here, adopting a two-layer architecture---similar to the connector rule abstraction proposed by Stevanovic and Wachter~\cite{StevanovicWachterRAISE}---could dynamically synthesize intermediate bridge predicates, linking environment-specific transport policies to high-level abstract logic rules.

Another critical extension involves multi-vector ingress analysis. The present reference evaluation models initial perimeter exposure primarily through an exposed, vulnerable jump server. Extending the input topology to incorporate alternate ingress surfaces, such as remote-access VPN endpoints, compromised engineering laptops, or supply-chain update channels, will enable comparative resilience scoring under diversified threat models.

Furthermore, dynamic risk metrics and probabilistic scoring represent a valuable enhancement. Although the derived graphs represent complete causal dependency trees, the evaluation currently treats exploitability as binary. Integrating dynamic probabilistic metrics, such as CVSS Exploitability sub-scores, the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS), and OT-specific operational disruption costs, into the MulVAL reasoning engine would enable quantitative risk prioritization and optimal remediation path identification.

Finally, the framework can be extended toward closed-loop automated hardening and prescriptive defense. While our counterfactual analysis demonstrated that eliminating perimeter vulnerabilities mitigates all downstream paths, future iterations could integrate automated synthesis of minimal-cut or shortest-path network interdiction remediation strategies. Formulating countermeasure selection as a network interdiction game or min-max security game would allow security teams to identify optimal defense policies against worst-case adversaries. Solving these games via Integer Programming, Answer Set Optimization (ASO), or Maximum Satisfiability (MaxSAT) formulations would enable the framework to prescribe the mathematically minimal set of firewall adjustments or host patches required to sever all viable paths to Layer~0 assets.
